At six months old, the twins sat in their high chairs side by side—trays locked, safety straps snug against their soft bellies. The medical schedule on Amerigo’s datapad glowed softly in the dim kitchen light of the habitat module: Introduction of solid foods. Day one. Mashed peas. He had prepared the mush himself, carefully spooning the reconstituted MRE paste into two tiny bowls. The peas had the dull olive color of something that had never seen real sunlight. He stirred them once more, testing the temperature against the inside of his wrist the way the parenting manuals insisted, even though the manuals had been written for children who were not… whatever his daughters were becoming.
Electra got the first bite.
He lifted the spoon to her mouth. She accepted it with the wary suspicion all babies reserved for green things—lips parting just enough, tongue advancing cautiously. Then her face scrunched in a full-body rejection: tiny nose wrinkling, mouth puckering as if she’d tasted something foul. A thin dribble of green escaped the corner of her lips and slid down her chin. Her whole torso recoiled, shoulders hunching, fists opening and closing in protest.
Amerigo laughed, the sound softer than he expected. “Not a fan, huh?”
He turned to Celeste, spoon already loaded with a fresh portion.
And stopped.
Celeste’s face was identical. Same scrunched nose. Same puckered mouth. Same expression of pure, unadulterated disgust. The same thin line of tension in her jaw. Even the same faint flush rising along her cheekbones.
She hadn’t eaten anything yet.
Amerigo’s hand froze, the spoon hovering halfway to her lips. A single drop of pea mush trembled at the edge of the metal and fell onto the tray with a soft plip.
Coincidence, he told himself. Just a coincidence. Babies make faces. They mirror each other. It’s nothing.
He tried again, this time surprising Electra while she was distracted by a shadow crossing the ceiling—some automated cleaning drone drifting past the viewport. He brought the spoon in from the side. She startled: eyes going wide, a tiny gasp catching in her throat, whole body flinching backward against the high-chair straps.
Beside her, Celeste startled too.
Same timing. Same wide eyes. Same tiny gasp. Same abrupt lean away from an invisible threat.
Amerigo set the spoon down carefully on the tray. He looked from one face to the other. Both stared back at him with identical expressions of baby confusion and growing wariness, as if he was the one acting strange. Electra’s left hand twitched. Celeste’s left hand twitched in the same fraction of a second. Electra’s gaze flicked toward the pea bowl. Celeste’s gaze followed a heartbeat later, though she had never turned her head.
“Okay,” he said aloud. The word felt too loud in the quiet kitchen. “That’s weird.”
But he didn’t tell anyone. Not yet. What would he say? I think my daughters might be sharing experiences somehow? It sounded insane. He was a scientist. He needed more data. He needed controlled variables. He needed to stop inventing ghosts in the nursery.

He wiped Electra’s chin with a soft cloth, then Celeste’s, though there was nothing on Celeste’s face to wipe. The motion was automatic. His mind was already racing ahead, cataloguing every previous anomaly he had tried to dismiss: the synchronized cries that began and ended within the same second even when the twins were in separate rooms; the identical sleep cycles that no amount of staggered feeding schedules could disrupt; the way they never seemed to need to look at each other to know what the other was doing, as if the space between their bodies simply did not exist for them.
Twin intuition, he told himself again, the phrase worn smooth from repetition. Nothing more.
He set the pea bowls aside. The green mush looked suddenly unappetizing even to him. Instead he reached for the second container he had prepared earlier that morning—banana-strawberry, a pale pink puree that smelled faintly of something almost like real fruit. The nutritional analysis on the packet claimed it was gentler, sweeter, more likely to be accepted on a first solid-food trial. He had meant it as a backup. Now it felt like a necessary experiment.
He loaded a clean spoon and offered it to Celeste first.
She accepted the spoon with the same cautious curiosity Electra had shown the peas. The tip of her tongue touched the puree. For a moment her expression remained neutral. Then her eyes brightened. The scrunch vanished. Her mouth worked thoughtfully, tasting, evaluating. A small, surprised smile curved her lips. She leaned forward, lips parting for more.
Amerigo felt something cold slide down the back of his neck.
He turned to Electra.
She was already reaching for the spoon.
Not tentatively. Not with the wary suspicion of a six-month-old encountering a new flavor. She stretched both hands toward him, fingers opening and closing in clear, urgent demand, eyes fixed on the pink puree with the focused intensity of someone who already knew exactly what it tasted like and already knew she liked it. Her mouth was open. Her tongue moved as if anticipating the sweetness. There was no hesitation, no trial, no first impression. Only recognition.
Amerigo’s hand trembled as he brought the spoon to her lips. She accepted it eagerly, humming a tiny sound of satisfaction that was almost a coo. Beside her, Celeste made the same sound at the same pitch, though her mouth was empty.
He fed them both another spoonful. Then another. They ate in perfect parallel—chewing, swallowing, reaching, smiling—as if the two bodies were executing a single shared program. When Electra’s nose wrinkled at a slightly thicker bit of banana, Celeste’s nose wrinkled in the same instant. When Celeste’s eyes drifted half-closed in contentment, Electra’s did the same.
Amerigo set the spoon down for the second time.
He stared at them.
The kitchen felt too quiet. The soft hum of the habitat’s life-support systems suddenly seemed very far away. He could hear his own pulse in his ears.
This isn’t twin intuition.

The thought arrived fully formed, unwanted, undeniable. He tried to push it back into the mental folder where he kept all the other anomalies, but the folder was overflowing. Synchronized startles. Identical expressions of disgust before any shared stimulus. Anticipatory reaching for a flavor only one of them had tasted. The way their breathing had fallen into the same rhythm the moment the banana-strawberry touched Celeste’s tongue.
He stood, pacing the short distance between the counter and the high chairs. His datapad lay on the table. Without conscious decision he picked it up, thumb already moving through the contact list toward the encrypted channel he used with Alex Cruiz—his oldest friend, his most trusted colleague, the only physician who had been present for the twins’ unusual conception and birth. Alex would listen. Alex would not immediately call him unstable. Alex might even have a theory that did not require Amerigo to rewrite everything he thought he knew about consciousness and individuality.
His thumb hovered over the initiate icon.
The soft pink light of the holo-call interface waited.
He looked at the twins again. Celeste was studying a speck of puree on her tray with deep concentration. Electra was studying the same speck on her own tray with the same concentration, though the speck on Electra’s tray was in a different place. Or perhaps it was the same speck, somehow. The idea made his stomach turn.
What would he even say?
Alex, I think my daughters are one person in two bodies. I think they share a single consciousness. I think when one of them tastes something, the other already knows.
It sounded like the opening line of a breakdown. It sounded like the kind of thing that got parental fitness evaluations triggered and children placed under observation. It sounded like the kind of claim that ended careers and invited questions about the experimental protocols that had brought the twins into existence in the first place.
Amerigo’s thumb moved away from the icon. He locked the datapad and set it face-down on the counter.
Not yet.
He needed more data. He needed patterns that could not be explained away. He needed to be certain that what he was seeing was not simply the desperate pattern-seeking of a tired father who had spent too many nights alone with two infants and too many unanswered questions.
He returned to the high chairs. Both girls watched him with the same calm, assessing gaze. He cleaned their faces and hands with practiced motions, unstrapped them one at a time, and carried them to the soft play mat in the living area. They settled side by side, already reaching for the same soft toy at the same moment, already turning their heads toward the same faint sound from the environmental systems.
Amerigo stood over them for a long time.
He filed the morning’s observations away—in the same mental folder where he had already placed the synchronized cries, the identical sleep cycles, the way they never seemed to need to look at each other to know what the other was doing. He labeled the folder carefully, clinically: Anomalous twin behaviors. Pending further observation.
Twin intuition, he told himself one last time, though the words had lost whatever comfort they once held.
But he saved the peas for another day. And he did not call Alex. Not yet.
