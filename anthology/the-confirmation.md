Amerigo watched his daughters' anticipation radiate through the exam room as Nurse Tim Alverson ushered them into the examination room. Amerigo took in the familiar details—the textured panels with their calming artificial wood grain, the soft hum of air filters, and the faint scent of antiseptic softened by something floral. Alex had always insisted a doctor’s office should feel less like a clinic and more like a place of comfort, and it showed; the small space felt more like a cozy study than a medical facility.  

Tim finished taking the twins’ vitals and glanced at his display. “As always, your measurements are identical—pulse at ninety-two, respiration at twenty-one, O₂ and blood pressure perfect and in sync.” He smiled. “You’re both small for seven—just 21.5 kg and 1.1 m, likely from being born so early—but your vital signs are excellent other than your usual high temp. The doc will be with you shortly.”  

“Goodbye. It was nice seeing you again, Nurse Alverson,” they said in dual harmony.  

Soon, Dr. Alex Cruiz’s presence filled the room, his kind tan eyes crinkling with warmth. “Salutations, dear ladies, and Amerigo. What brings you here today?”  

Their faces lit up. “Hi, Uncle Doc\! I’m here to see you, of course\!”  

Amerigo’s voice carried both affection and bewilderment. “Hi, Alex. My daughters insist they’re one person with a single mind spanning two bodies. I’m pretty much convinced, but I need your expertise to confirm it.” He recounted incidents—like when Celeste felt a tickle on Electra’s foot. “They complete each other’s sentences and respond as one entity. Look at their charts—identical weight, height, and vitals. It’s as if they’re two halves of a whole.”  

“I’ve noticed similar events. Let’s try something.” Alex stepped toward a drawer and returned with flashcards. “Celeste, could you turn around and face the wall?”  

When she complied, he began showing cards to Electra. The flick of the cardboard was loud in the quiet room. “What do you see, Celeste?”  

Celeste, facing the wall, answered instantly. “A red bicycle.”  

Amerigo’s pulse quickened. *She couldn’t see the card—could she?*  

*“*A yellow flower.” “A blue umbrella.” “Three cats.”  

Amerigo leaned forward, the last remnants of doubt cracking with each correct answer. “That’s... that’s impossible.”  

Alex’s expression was somewhere between wonder and clinical fascination. He held up five more cards in rapid succession. Every answer came back perfect, instantaneous, and spoken by the child who couldn’t possibly see them.  

“Now switch. Electra, turn to the wall.”  

The pattern repeated flawlessly.  

Alex set down the cards, his hands slightly unsteady. “This is extraordinary, but I want to try one more thing.” He gestured to an open space in the exam room. “Celeste, could you do some jumping jacks for me? Just one of your bodies—the other should stay completely still.”  

Celeste stepped forward while Electra remained by the chair. “How many?”  

“Twenty should be plenty. Nice and quick.” Alex attached simple pulse monitors to both her wrists, the displays glowing softly. “Your resting heart rates are both at 92 beats per minute. Perfectly synchronized, as always.”  

Celeste began jumping—arms up, legs apart, then together again in the familiar rhythm. Her left body stood motionless, watching with mild curiosity.  

Five jumps in, both monitors began climbing. 95... 98... 102...  

“Keep going,” Alex encouraged, his eyes fixed on the displays.  

Ten jumps. Both hearts: 108 bpm.  

Fifteen jumps. 115 bpm.  

Twenty. Celeste stopped, breathing hard. Both monitors read 122 beats per minute.  

“Now the interesting part,” Alex murmured. He waited, watching both displays as her heart rates gradually declined together. 118... 112... 105...  

Amerigo leaned forward. “Her resting body’s heart rate increased even though it didn’t move.”  

“Exactly.” Alex carefully removed the monitors. “Girls, which body feels tired right now?”  

“Both,” she said immediately, both voices slightly breathless. “My left body didn’t jump, but it feels like it did. Tired legs, faster heartbeat—everything.”  

“Because the autonomic response is controlled by one brain,” Alex said, more to himself than anyone else. “Electra’s brain, most likely, given the data. It regulates both her cardiovascular systems and both respiratory rates. When one body exercises, the shared autonomic system responds to the demand—even though only one body is creating that demand.”  

He looked at Amerigo with something like awe. “This isn’t just synchronized movement or shared sensation. This is unified physiology. One autonomic control center managing two complete circulatory systems.”  

“Is that... dangerous?” Amerigo asked, concerned.  

“Actually, it might be protective,” Alex said thoughtfully. “Redundancy with centralized control. If one body’s heart fails, the other brain’s motor control could potentially compensate. Though that’s speculation—we’ve never seen anything like this before.”  

He turned to the computer. “Utara, I assume you’ve been listening. Have you observed anything like this?”  

“Yes, Dr. Cruiz,” she answered, “I have been observing Celectra as the Colony Educator while teaching her. I believe she is correct about being one person. When I recently insisted both bodies take separate tests, both came up with the same correct and incorrect answers, even when I mixed up the question order.”  

“The same *wrong* answers?” Amerigo interjected.  

“Yes. Rare, but always identical. The way she talks, using either mouth or both, may be comical in movies, but it’s absolute reality with her. The most definitive test: having one read random text silently while the other spoke aloud. No matter which does what, the speaker is always in sync with the reader. I don’t have all the answers, but I believe her.”  

Alex’s brow furrowed. “Wait—what did you call her?”  

“Celectra. It was her idea to merge her names about two years ago.”  

The room fell silent. Both faces brightened, watching the adults expectantly.  

Alex tested the name aloud. “Celectra.” He looked at the girl—*girls? girl?*—and something shifted in his expression. “That’s... actually quite elegant. It honors both bodies while affirming the singular identity.”  

Amerigo felt something twist in his chest. His daughter had been telling him for years, and he’d nodded along, but hearing Alex say it—hearing Utara confirm it with data—made it suddenly, uncomfortably real.  

“Why didn’t you say something sooner?” Alex asked Utara, frustration touching his voice.  

“After I heard her tell Amerigo, I started testing. I didn’t wish to speak until I was certain. There’s nothing anyone can do about it, and it isn’t a problem. On the contrary, it’s a tremendous asset. She has twice the brainpower of the average human, not to mention extra hands and feet.”  

Alex turned to Celectra. “Is that what you’d like us to call you?”  

Both faces nodded eagerly. “Yes\! I’ve been Celectra for a long time, but most people still call me ‘the twins’ or ‘Electra and Celeste”.”  

“Then Celectra it is.” Alex glanced at Amerigo. “If that’s all right with you?”  

Amerigo swallowed hard. “I... yes. Yes, of course.” He tried the name himself. “Celectra.”  

Her dual smiles could have lit the entire station.  

Alex pulled up a holographic display. “I’m still not sure this is neurologically possible. Brainwaves don’t extend beyond the scalp—it’s impossible for thoughts to just beam out and connect.”  

Celectra piped up, both voices in perfect unison. “I think I know. When I was in my mommy’s tummy, my heads were really close together, so my brains could talk easily. But when my bodies moved around, my heads got further apart, so my brains had to shout louder to stay connected. After I was born and people moved me apart for baths and stuff, my brains had to push out even harder.”  

Alex blinked, visibly stunned. He leaned back, rubbing his chin. “That... actually makes sense. In the first trimester, your skulls would have been soft and barely formed. Almost nothing to block electrical signals between your brains. And since fetal brains are so plastic, any tiny pathway could strengthen with use. When your heads moved apart, it was like exercising a muscle—building a stronger bridge.”  

“Electrical signals? So I have like... Celectricity bouncing between my brains? Like Niamh and Ciara getting some of each other’s radiation?”  

Alex chuckled. “Yeah, maybe something like that. Maybe your brains are so identical that each thought it was talking to other parts of itself.”  

Amerigo’s brow furrowed. “So it started because her skulls were still soft and unfinished, and the brains kept reinforcing the link as they grew?” Worry crept into his voice. “What does it mean for her now? Could that bridge ever break if she’s separated physically? Or could the connection itself cause problems as she keeps developing?”  

Celectra froze. Not just a pause, but a mirror-stillness that made the air feel heavy. Both bodies drew arms around her knees, eyes wide. For the first time, Amerigo saw fear in both sets of eyes.  

Her voices were thin, almost pleading. “But... I’ve never been apart. Not really. What if—what if that’s why?”  

“I’m sorry, sweetheart. I didn’t mean—”  

“We won’t let anything happen to you,” Alex cut in firmly. “That’s exactly why we need to understand this better.”  

He returned his attention to the holographic brain scans, twin neural matrices glowing in false color. “We’ve been tracking her development since birth. When we thought Celeste and Electra were two separate people, we saw each brain had a corpus callosum nearly twice the normal density—unusually enhanced connectivity between hemispheres. We attributed their individual brilliance to that.”  

He highlighted regions across both scans. “But now we see the full picture: unprecedented neural synchronization between the two brains, signals aligning in real time. One brain, likely Electra’s based on the lack of activity in that part of Celeste’s brain, handles autonomic processes for both, explaining identical vitals. Their brilliance isn’t just from two enhanced brains—it’s synergistic intellect with full redundancy and emergent abilities.”  

Celectra exclaimed, both faces brightening up. “I’m a genius squared\!”  

“But why?” Amerigo asked, giving Celectra a thumbs-up.  

“The flare exposure during neural tube formation. We pulled the sensor logs from Utopis-Lab 3\. The station passed through the edge of Niamh’s coronal mass ejection for approximately eight minutes. Primarily high-energy protons and gamma radiation. Jenni’s estimated dose was around 1080millisieverts.”  

Amerigo’s jaw tightened at the memory. Sublethal, but significant. Especially for what had been growing inside her.  

“That radiation exposure hit during a critical window,” Alex said quietly. “Normally, you’d expect developmental defects or miscarriage. Instead, it rewired Celectra’s biology in three ways.” He gestured to the scans. “The radiation didn't just damage tissue—it rewired developmental pathways during neural tube formation. Normally, embryonic brains develop strict boundaries between individuals. But at that critical window, the proton exposure appears to have fused signaling channels between your daughters' developing cortices—creating a bridge where biology intended separation. Second, placental damage caused insufficiency, leading to prematurity at 1.3 kilograms each, and her small size now. Third, the radiation triggered a localized **apoptotic cascade**, halting the melanoblasts before they could reach the epidermis. They simply never arrived, leaving her with white hair and fragile skin. But the neuroectoderm of her optic cups had already differentiated; her irises were already locked into their milk chocolate hue, a biological relic of the week before the storm hit. Three miracles from one tragedy.”  

Amerigo swallowed. He had looked into those eyes a thousand times and never thought of them as anything but theirs—now hers.  

“Enhanced connectivity. Bilateral synchronization. The brains never had a chance to develop two separate consciousnesses. The result is an emergent phenomenon: two highly enhanced processors running a single, unified operating system. That’s why it’s not simply additive brilliance—it’s exponential. They literally evolved into a higher form of consciousness to survive the trauma. A forced evolutionary leap.”  

“You’re saying the radiation created her condition.”  

“I’m saying it created the conditions for it. But there are variables we can’t account for.” Alex pulled up genetic profiles. “Jenni’s mitochondrial DNA shows unusual repair enzyme expression—likely why she recovered faster than some others. The embryos’ positioning in utero, the specific gestational timing, even the station’s magnetic shielding creating uneven exposure patterns... Remove any single factor and you might get normal twins, damaged twins, or no pregnancy at all.”  

“Have you checked if anyone else—”  

“Jenni was the only pregnant person on that mission. The only data point we have.” Alex confirmed. “Dr. Chen on Utopis-Lab 3 confirmed that no other pregnancies were detected within the subsequent two cycles. In fact, everyone that was on board at the time has been confirmed sterile.” Alex’s expression grew somber. “Which brings us to the other question you haven’t asked in seven years.”  

Amerigo’s jaw clenched.  

“Why Jenni died in childbirth.” Alex pulled up medical files—prenatal scans, placental anomalies, and the frantic delivery room moments. “The radiation that enhanced Celectra’s neural development also damaged Jenni’s placental tissue. It caused subtle vascular weakening and restricted blood flow, stunting growth in utero.”  

Alex’s voice softened, heavy with regret. “When labor began early, the weakened placenta started to detach. The hemorrhaging was catastrophic. We rushed her to surgery to save the twins, but the cascade of complications...”  

“You couldn’t stop it,” Amerigo finished, his voice hollow. He’d heard fragments before, but never the full weight of why. Why Jenni, who’d survived the flare exposure and who’d carried their tiny twins to thirty-two weeks, had been lost in that final, terrible hour.  

“The flare gave you Celectra and took Jenni. Cause and effect, but through mechanisms we still don’t fully understand. I wish I had better answers, more data. But this is frontier medicine—we’re making educated guesses with sample sizes of one.”  

Amerigo stared at the brain scans. “Can you predict what will happen as she develops? If the connection will strengthen, weaken, or if there will be complications—”  

“No. Her electromagnetic sensitivity is already beyond anything we can properly measure. Every milestone she hits, we’re in uncharted territory.” Alex paused. “We don’t know what we don’t know. If we try to map this too aggressively, dissect it, replicate it... we could destroy what makes her functional while trying to understand what makes her unique.”  

Amerigo thought of Jenni, her hand in his during those last minutes, whispering, “Take care of them,” before the light left her eyes.  

“So we protect her,” Amerigo said quietly. “From anyone who sees her as an experiment instead of a person.”  

“Exactly.” Alex nodded firmly. “We know the *what*—radiation exposure during critical development created unprecedented neural architecture. We just don’t understand the *how*, and we may never fully understand it. But she’s thriving. She’s healthy, brilliant, and happy. Sometimes that has to be enough.”  

Alex turned back to Celectra, his expression softening. “I’d say the safest assumption is that this connection isn’t a flaw—it’s part of who you are. I recommend you see Dr. Zoë Kret on Ares. She’s better equipped to monitor your psychological development and ensure nothing concerning develops as you grow.”  

Amerigo stood, extending his hand. “All right. Thanks for your time.”  

Alex shook warmly. “I’ll have Tim schedule an appointment with Dr. Zoë. Are there any days you won’t be able to make it?”  

Amerigo smiled. “Anytime works. I’m senior enough to leave work whenever I need to. And Celectra can do her studies anytime. Right, sweetheart?”  

“Yes, Daddy,” she said with a big grin on each angelic face.  

Celectra beamed, both sets of eyes shining. “See, Daddy? I told you it’s just me.” She added more softly, “I like it when people say it’s not a problem.”  

Amerigo’s expression softened, and for the first time in the conversation, a small smile touched his face. He knelt down and pulled both of her small bodies to him in a double hug. He finally let himself believe it—she was Celectra, not Celeste and Electra. One extraordinary daughter.  

“You’re right, sweetheart. It is just you, and you’re more than enough. I wouldn’t trade you for anything.” He glanced at Alex, concern still flickering but tempered now with acceptance. “I just want to make sure you’ll always be safe, no matter how different you are.”  

Amerigo thought of Jenni, her courage in those early months of pregnancy, and hoped she’d be proud of their extraordinary daughter. Maybe this was her gift—a child who defied every boundary.  

Alex smiled. “I suppose we should name it... let’s see... one mind spanning two bodies. Latin would be Una mens, duo corpora… or Greek, Mononous disomas. That sounds better.”  

Celectra cringed at both. Amerigo saw it too—the way it landed like a diagnosis instead of a description.  

“Eww. I’m not sick. How about something better? Like... celectrics.”  

“That doesn’t mean you have a disease,” Alex consoled. “It’s just a name for a condition, like being tall or short, or even a genius or athletic.”  

After saying goodbye to Dr. Cruiz, Amerigo and Celectra left his office and headed down the upward-curving corridor toward their quarters. As they walked, Celectra looked up at her father with love beaming in her eyes. “Thank you for calling me ‘Celectra,’ Daddy\!”  

Amerigo squeezed both her hands gently. “You’re welcome, sweetheart. I should have listened sooner.”
