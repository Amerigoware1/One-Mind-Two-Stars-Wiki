Amerigo sat heavily in the middle of the sofa, absently cupping the four little feet in his lap between his hands. Celectra lay with a head on each arm of the sofa, each with a holopad synced to the same page of a book. Just this morning, Alex had confirmed what Amerigo had begun to suspect: Celectra wasn't twins, Celeste and Electra, but one person—a single consciousness in two bodies.

The confirmation was a relief, but the explanation—radiation-induced neural architecture at the cost of Jenni's life—left him hollow. He felt the phantom weight of her hand on his shoulder, the ghost of her scientific curiosity that would have exploded with wonder at Celectra's reality. He was finally able to name the miracle, but naming it only sharpened the wound.

He closed his eyes against the sterile gray of the cabin walls. Seventeen years since they'd woken from cryosleep to this new world. But some memories refused to fade. He tried to steer his thoughts to happier times, but her absence now felt more real than ever, a shadow tied to the miracle sprawled across his lap.

\*\*\*

Amerigo's mind drifted to the wedding—a memory like a sun-warmed stone in his hand. Seventeen years since they'd woken from cryosleep to this new world, but Earth's blue sky remained vivid behind his eyes, and Jenni stood radiant in a simple dress, her laughter brighter than the bells that rang out after they said their vows.

He could still see her in perfect detail: the jasmine tucked behind her ear, the way her dark hair caught the afternoon sun, those hazel eyes that always saw more than they should. She’d written her own vows—of course she had, practical Jenni, who never did anything by half measures.

<!-- page -->

“Amerigo,” she’d said, voice steady despite the tears shimmering, “I promise to explore the universe with you. To dig through asteroid cores and lava tubes and whatever weird rocks we find out there. To build something bigger than both of us. And to always, *always* remind you to eat when you’re lost in your calculations.”

The guests had laughed. He’d kissed her before the officiant gave permission, earning more laughter and applause.

They’d married in a small garden in New Mexico, surrounded by friends who teased them about being “star-crossed” for choosing a life bound for a binary star system—or beyond if necessary. The air smelled of jasmine and desert sage. String lights swayed overhead as they danced, her head tucked perfectly against his chest, their future stretching out like the cosmos—endless, uncharted, theirs.

“We’re gonna build a whole world together, Migo,” she had whispered against his shoulder. “Just you wait.”

Alex had been his best man, already half in love with Dee but too stubborn to admit it. They’d all been so *young* then, bright-eyed volunteers signing up for humanity’s latest grand adventure. Forty-five light-years from Earth. A one-way trip. But Jenni had grabbed his hand during the recruitment presentation, squeezed it tight, and whispered, “Let’s go.”

How could he have said no?

\*\*\*

Their honeymoon wasn’t a relaxing beach trip; it was an adventure—the first of many they’d planned together. They spent two weeks on the Big Island of Hawaii, but instead of resorts, they explored the vast geological history of the island.

<!-- page -->

Amerigo remembered the lava tubes near Kīlauea most vividly. The cool, damp air. The crushing silence. They had to duck low, stepping over sharp volcanic rock, the beam of their flashlights swallowed by massive black cavern walls. It felt like walking through the planet’s dark, beating heart.

Jenni hadn’t been scared; she was *fascinated*. She traced the striations in the rock with her gloved hand, reading it like a book. “You see these flow patterns? This tube formed over centuries. Lava ran underneath while the surface cooled and hardened above. Like veins carrying a planet’s blood.”

She’d looked at him in the darkness, eyes bright with wonder. “Think about it, ‘Migo. Even the most destructive forces create pathways. Lava destroys everything, but it leaves behind these spaces—conduits for water, shelter for animals, eventually. Death becomes infrastructure.”

He’d kissed her then, tasting dust and chapstick and possibility.

“That’s us,” she’d said, laughing against his mouth. “We’re going to tunnel through the unknown and leave pathways behind us. For the people who come after.”

That trip, plunging into Earth’s history, had cemented their shared love for the frontier and the unknown. It prepared them for the cosmos in ways they hadn’t understood then—taught them that beauty and danger were often the same thing, that the scariest places held the most profound discoveries.

If only he’d known how prophetic her words would be.

\*\*\*

They’d delayed starting a family, caught up in the chaos of New Eden’s founding. The cryosleep journey had taken seventy-eight years. They’d woken in a system that had never seen human footprints, with work that consumed every waking hour.

<!-- page -->

Jenni’s deployments took her across the system—surveying moons, mapping resources, always chasing the next horizon. She’d comm him from Oscar or Oisín or one of the smaller satellites, her face pixelated but smiling, showing him core samples like they were treasures.

“Look at this, ‘Migo—platinum group metals, right where the models predicted. We’re going to be *rich*.” She’d laughed at her own joke. Nobody came this far for money.

For four years they waited to try for children. Not out of fear, but because the colony needed them and the timing never felt right. They were helping build the infrastructure for a new world—water reclamation, atmospheric processors, and mining operations. Critical work that couldn’t wait.

Then they’d tried for five years.

Amerigo remembered the first negative test. Jenni’s forced smile, her determinedly cheerful voice: “It’s fine. It happens. We’ll try again next month.”

The second. The third. By the sixth, she’d stopped smiling. Just stared at the negative indicator, silent, her hand pressed against her flat stomach like she could will life into being through sheer force of hope.

By the tenth, they’d stopped counting.

“Maybe it’s not meant to be,” she’d said one night, curled against him in their quarters on Utopis. Outside the porthole, Ares spun past in shades of rust and shadow. “Maybe the colony is our baby. Maybe that’s enough.”

He’d held her while she cried, feeling utterly helpless. His brilliant, brave wife, who could read a planet’s history in its rocks, who could calculate orbital mechanics in her head—and there was nothing either of them could do about this.

“It’ll happen when it happens,” he’d told her, words that felt hollow even as he spoke them. “Or it won’t. Either way, I’ve got you.”

<!-- page -->

She’d nodded against his chest, but he felt the dampness of her tears soaking through his shirt.

After that, they stopped trying. Stopped planning. Just lived their lives, did their work, and loved each other. If it happened, it happened.

\*\*\*

The call came through at 0300 station time.

Jenni’s face appeared on his holo-display, but there was no joy in her expression. Just hollow shock and desperate, fragile hope.

“‘Migo,” she said, and her voice cracked on his name. “I have news, and it’s all bad, except for the beginning. I’m pregnant. With twins.”

Amerigo’s heart stalled. Twins. The word barely registered before the rest of her sentence crashed down, killing the nascent joy.

“But something happened,” she continued, words coming faster now like she needed to get them all out before she lost her nerve. “There was a stellar flare. A high-energy blast from Niamh. We got caught in it—eight minutes of exposure. Dr. Chen said... 1080 millisieverts, ‘Migo. That’s bad. That’s really bad at this stage.”

The bottom dropped out of his world.

“How bad?” he asked the blurry display.

“They can’t say anything for certain. Dr. Chen said standard outcomes at this exposure level are... not good. Developmental defects. Miscarriage.” She swallowed hard. “But she also said I have unusual repair enzymes. That maybe—*maybe*—they’ll be okay.”

Amerigo’s hands shook as he gripped the edge of his desk.

“I’ll be there in four days. We’ll figure this out together.”

“I’m scared.” Her voice was so small, so unlike his fearless Jenni, who dove into lava tubes and surveyed hostile moons without hesitation. “I’m so scared.”

“I’m not giving up on them,” she said fiercely. “I don’t care what the odds are. They’re fighters. They have to be. They’re *ours*.”

<!-- page -->

He nodded, unable to speak past the lump in his throat.

“Four days,” she repeated. “I’ll see you in four days.”

The display went dark, and Amerigo sat in his quarters, staring at nothing, trying to process what he’d just learned. Twins. Radiation exposure. Uncertain odds.

He thought about Jenni’s words in the lava tube: *even the most destructive forces create pathways.*

He prayed she was right.

\*\*\*

Twenty-six weeks later—eight weeks too early—the cramps began just after midnight.

Amerigo had insisted on taking her to the med bay himself, one arm around her as they hurried through the dim corridors. The medics took Jenni inside while he waited just outside the curtain, trying to stay calm as monitors beeped and voices murmured in low urgency.

At some point, exhaustion caught up with him. He sat on a bench, head in his hands, and must have drifted off.

He woke to the blare of the station alarm.

“Medical emergency, Utopis Station birthing center. Dr. Cruiz to emergency obstetrics immediately.”

Amerigo’s blood turned to ice. He stumbled to his feet and ran across the waiting room toward the sound, still half-asleep, barefoot in a bathrobe, heart pounding. The medical bay was chaos—Alex shouting orders, nurses prepping equipment, and Alex already in surgical scrubs with blood on his gloves.

“Amerigo—” Alex said into the overhead microphone as he worked, face grim. “Placental abruption. She’s hemorrhaging. We’re taking them now.”

“The babies—”

“Too early, but viable. It’s Jenni I’m worried about.”

<!-- page -->

They wouldn’t let him into the surgical suite. He paced outside, hands shaking, praying to gods he didn’t believe in. Through the small window, he caught flashes: Jenni’s pale face, the frantic movements of the medical team, the harsh lights that made everything look too stark, too real.

Then a thin, warbling cry. And another.

A nurse emerged, guiding an incubator with two impossibly small infants nestled together for warmth inside.

“Mr. Carisco? Your daughters.”

He stared at them—these tiny scraps of humanity. White hair, shock-white, as if radiation had bleached it before they ever breathed air. Transparent skin. But when their eyelids fluttered open, their eyes were dark.

Like his.

“Are they—” He couldn’t say it.

“Alive. Breathing. 1.3 kilograms each. We’ll monitor them closely, but for now, they’re stable.”

“And Jenni?”

The nurse’s expression shuttered. “Dr. Cruiz is still with her.”

They let him into the recovery room twenty minutes later. He knew before he saw her face.

Jenni lay pale against white sheets, tubes, and monitors, making her seem smaller than she was. Her hazel eyes found his, and she smiled—that same bright smile from their wedding day, only dimmed now, fading like a star at dawn.

“‘Migo,” she whispered. “Did you see them? Our girls?”

He couldn’t speak. Could only nod, tears streaming down his face as he took her hand.

“Two,” she said, wonder in her voice despite everything. “Can you believe it? Two little miracles.” Her grip was weak, slipping. “Promise me something.”

“Anything. Jenni, please, just hold on—”

<!-- page -->

“Promise me you’ll tell them about me.” The urgency in her voice made him freeze. She knew. She knew she was dying. “Not just the big stuff. Tell them about the time I accidentally vented the entire sample bay trying to open a window.” A weak laugh that turned into a cough. “Tell them I loved them before I ever met them. Tell them their mommy wanted to see the stars with them.”

“You will,” he choked out, denial thick in his throat. “You’ll be there. You’ll tell them yourself. You’ll—”

“Take care of them,” she interrupted, using the last of her strength. Her eyes locked onto his with fierce intensity. “They’re special, ‘Migo. I felt it. Even in the womb. They’re... connected somehow. Different. Don’t let anyone make them feel wrong for it. Promise me.”

“I promise. Jenni, I promise, but you have to stay—please, stars, you have to stay—”

Her grip loosened. Her eyes drifted past him, seeing something he couldn’t, something beyond the sterile walls and recycled air.

“The stars are so beautiful from here,” she murmured, voice barely a breath.

And then she was gone.

Amerigo felt the exact moment her hand went slack in his. Felt the universe tilt and crack and shatter into something he didn’t recognize. He heard himself making sounds—animal sounds, grief too raw for words—while Alex’s hands tried to pull him away and nurses moved around Jenni’s body with terrible, practiced efficiency.

She was thirty years old. She’d survived a forty-five light-year journey across the cosmos, surveyed a dozen moons, dove into lava tubes, and mapped asteroid fields. And she’d died here, on a space station, bringing life into the world.

The injustice of it made him want to break something.

Instead, he went to see his daughters.

\*\*\*

<!-- page -->

Seven days later, the medics cleared him to take the girls home.

He sat on the floor of his quarters, two impossibly small bodies in bassinets beside him, and realized he had absolutely no idea what to do.

They were so tiny—1.3 kilograms each, half the size of normal newborns. Their skin was translucent, veins visible beneath like maps of rivers on an alien world. Their white hair stuck up in wispy tufts. They looked fragile enough to shatter at a touch.

He was terrified to hold them.

He’d attended every Lamaze class with Jenni, memorized the nurses’ instructions, and practiced swaddling with dolls in preparation. But none of it had prepared him for *this*—for being solely responsible for two lives this small, this vulnerable, while drowning in grief so profound he could barely breathe.

One of them whimpered—a sound so soft he almost missed it. Then the other joined in, their cries synchronizing into a heartbreaking duet.

His hands shook as he reached into the bassinet.

“Don’t drop them,” he whispered to himself. “Don’t break them. Please, stars, don’t let me break them.”

He managed to get one—Electra, according to the bracelet still on her ankle—cradled against his chest. She was warm and impossibly light, like holding concentrated starlight. Her tiny fist curled against his collarbone.

The crying stopped.

“Hey,” he whispered, voice breaking. “Hey, little one. I’m your daddy. I’m... I don’t know what I’m doing. Your mommy would have been so much better at this. She would have—”

He couldn’t finish. He just held his daughter and wept, this tiny miracle that had cost him everything.

A knock at the door. Nurse Williams, still in her scrubs, carrying a bag of supplies.

“Thought you might need help,” she said gently.

<!-- page -->

He nodded, unable to speak.

She sat with him through that first terrible night, showing him how to hold them properly and how to tell when they were hungry versus tired versus just needing comfort. Her hands were sure where his shook, her voice calm where his broke.

“Talk to them,” she instructed. “They know your voice. They heard you and Jenni together for months. They know you.”

He tried. His voice cracked. “Your mommy... she loved you so much. She…” The words dissolved into sobs.

In the silence that followed, with both girls now nestled against him—one on each arm, their tiny chests rising and falling in eerie unison—the only thing that came to mind was the melody he’d hummed to Jenni in their last quiet nights together.

His voice, rough and wavering, stumbled into the notes: With you by my side, the world feels right... Love’s in the air, come what may. It wasn’t much—just a fragment of the song he’d written for her Parisian dreams—but the girls stilled, their eyes flickering open in the dim light, as if recognizing the promise already woven into their shared pulse.

And in that hush, with their warmth seeping into his skin like the first breath of spring, Amerigo felt it: love blooming unbidden, fierce and fragile, pulling him under as surely as the Seine’s current.

Williams didn’t tell him to stop crying. She just sat beside him, her slightly wrinkled hand resting on Amerigo’s shoulder, while he held his daughters and grieved for the woman who’d given everything to bring them into the world.

\*\*\*

By week two, Amerigo had resorted to colored ribbons tied around their right ankles. Gold for Electra. Blue for Celeste.

Without them, he genuinely couldn’t tell them apart—same white hair, same chocolate eyes, same tiny features, same expressions. They even cried in the same key, their sounds overlapping until he couldn’t distinguish who needed what.

Alex found it hilarious when he came to check on them. “You know most parents can tell their twins apart, right?”

“Most parents have twins that are actually different people,” Amerigo shot back without thinking, then froze. “I mean—”

But the joke landed wrong. Because something in the way they synchronized everything—their sleep cycles, their hunger, their cries—wasn’t normal even for twins.

Even this early, there were signs.

\*\*\*

<img src="{{ '/assets/images/gallery/baby-celectra.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Celectra Eating" style="width:30%;">

At six months, Amerigo fed them in high chairs side by side, trying to introduce solid foods per the developmental schedule Williams had given him.

He gave Electra a spoonful of mashed peas. Her face scrunched up in disgust, her mouth puckering like she’d tasted poison.

And Celeste, on the other side of the high chair divider, made the exact same expression—without having been offered any food yet.

Amerigo froze, spoon halfway to Celeste’s mouth.

*Coincidence,* he told himself. *Just a coincidence.*

He tried again, this time surprising Electra with the spoon while she was distracted. She startled, eyes going wide.

Celeste startled, too. Same timing, same wide eyes, same tiny gasp.

“What in the...” He looked between them. Both stared at him with identical expressions of baby confusion and growing wariness. “Okay. That’s weird.”

<!-- page -->

But he didn’t tell anyone. Not yet. Because what would he say? *I think my daughters might be sharing experiences somehow*? It sounded insane.

He filed it away in his mind with all the other oddities he’d noticed, telling himself it was just the strangeness of having twins, of being a new parent, of trying to navigate fatherhood while drowning in grief.

\*\*\*

When they were two, Amerigo stood in the bathroom doorway, watching in confusion. Celeste, now wearing star-shaped gold earrings, had been playing in the living room. Electra, with amber earrings, had announced softly, “Potty time\!” and trotted off to the bathroom.

He’d followed Electra, ready to help. But before he got there, Celeste had abandoned her toys and was already walking toward the bathroom too—without a word being spoken, without any signal he could detect.

They both used their respective toilets, perfectly synchronized, and then both washed their hands, chattering to him but never to each other.

“Good job, girls\!” he said, trying to sound normal.

But inside, his scientist’s brain was cataloging: No verbal communication. No visible signals. Perfect synchronization. How?

\*\*\*

At three, they were in the hydroponics bay with Dee, supposedly learning about plants. But Dee had asked, “Celeste, can you and your sister water those tomatoes?”

Both bodies stopped moving. Turned. Fixed Dee with identical expressions of patient exasperation.

“I’m not two people,” she said, voices overlapping. “I’m just me.”

Dee glanced at Amerigo, uncertain. He shrugged and smiled. “Sorry, sweetie. Can you water the tomatoes?”

“Yes, Daddy.” And she did—one body holding the watering can, the other guiding it, moving together like a choreographed dance.

<!-- page -->

Afterward, Dee pulled him aside. “Amerigo... did you notice they said ‘I’m,’ not ‘we’re’?”

He had. But he’d dismissed it as linguistic confusion. Three-year-olds made grammar mistakes all the time.

Didn’t they?

By five, teachers noted they never spoke between themselves, yet moved in perfect unison—drawing identical pictures, solving puzzles as if one brain drove both bodies. At six, Amerigo began to wonder.

Alex and Dee saw their uniqueness, too. Alex, still carrying the weight of losing Jenni, adored them. He once gave them a broken comms unit, expecting a mess. Instead, one body dismantled it while the other sketched a fix, their movements eerily coordinated. “They’re brilliant,” Alex told Amerigo, shaking his head. Dee, gentler, braided their fragile, white hair in the hydroponics bay, sharing stories of Earth. Once, she watched one body watering plants while the other drew their leaves, perfectly matched. “They feel the world so deeply,” Dee said softly.

Now, Amerigo was seeing old memories in a new light. Celectra lived her single mind across two bodies like a birthright. Even now, resting and still. But Amerigo’s mind flashed back to her everyday life: the controlled chaos of her unique play. She was the unofficial ruler of the *Utopis* station’s sloping corridors. She didn’t just walk—she flowed. On the way to music, karate, or dance class, one body would run ahead, darting behind an air scrubber, while the other would pivot on a dime, laughing as it chased its own reflection.

<!-- page -->

Her feet were warm in his hands now, bare, toes curling against his palms—and the sight of them shook loose a memory so small he’d nearly lost it.

She’d been four, and had dressed herself for the first time, a victory she announced to the whole corridor outside their quarters. Both bodies stood there beaming, wiggling their toes in two pairs of matching sandals.

It took him a moment to see it. One body wore two left sandals. The other wore two rights. Each had one sandal on the wrong foot.

“Sweetheart,” he began, already smiling, “I think your shoes—”

Both heads dipped at the same instant. She’d seen it before he finished.

“Oh,” she said, in two voices, with the mild surprise of someone who’d buttoned a shirt wrong.

No discussion. Not so much as a glance between them. Each body bent and unbuckled the wrong-footed sandal, and the two sets of hands met in the space between, passing the shoes across like a relay baton. Left to left. Right to right. Whole again.

“There,” she said, and bolted down the corridor, four sandals slapping in perfect time.

He’d smiled and thought nothing of it. Now it read differently: not two sisters sorting out a mix-up, but one girl who’d dressed herself with one mind, made one mistake, and corrected it the way anyone shifts a shoe back to the right foot.

He remembered the time she reprogrammed the reclamatics to follow her like ducklings last year when she was 6\. One body stood beside a maintenance panel, fingers flying over the interface like she was tickling it into submission. Amerigo had thought it was a game. A prank. A coincidence. But now he saw it: the choreography. One body distracted the automated cleaning  bots—twirling, skipping, tossing a crumpled piece of paper like bait. The reclamatics, programmed to chase debris, followed her in a neat little line. Meanwhile, her other body rerouted their behavior protocols, assigning her own biosignature as their new “target object.” She didn’t need to speak. Didn’t need to signal. She just *knew* what her other self was doing. And when the reclamatics began trailing behind her like obedient ducklings, both bodies giggled in perfect unison. Amerigo had laughed, too, back then. But now, he relived it with awe.

Another time, Celectra had convinced Aunt Dee to teach her how to make real pancakes. In the Cruiz’s kitchen, one body smoothly beat the batter with a whisk while the other, at the counter’s edge, delicately portioned and folded in melted butter—a seamless, yet messy, division of labor resulting in perfectly proportioned ingredients in the mix and two sets of small, flour-dusted footprints leading away from the crime scene.

They moved like two ends of the same magnificent piece of string, never crossing, always connected, owning the space as if the entire station were their private playroom.

\*\*\*

Just yesterday, though it felt like a lifetime, Amerigo had watched her solve a calculus problem that Utara had given her as a “challenge”—high school-level mathematics. Problems he himself struggled with.

<!-- page -->

One body wrote the equations in smooth, confident strokes. The other sketched a visual proof, translating abstract numbers into geometric shapes. Both worked simultaneously on different aspects of the same problem, never consulting, never checking with each other, never even glancing between bodies.

When she finished, she’d looked up at him with both faces, grinning. “That was fun\! Can I have another?”

He’d stared at the completed work, his heart pounding with a mixture of pride and something else—something like fear, or awe, or both.

“Girls, when you do that—when both of you are working on the same problem—what does it feel like?”

Both heads tilted in perfect synchronization, considering. The gesture was so precisely mirrored it made his skin prickle.

“It feels like... me thinking,” she said slowly, as if searching for words to describe something that should be obvious. “Just me. With extra hands.” A pause, those chocolate eyes—*shaped like Jenni’s eyes*, he thought with a pang—studying him with concern. “Why, Daddy? Does it feel different when you think?”

And that’s when he had called Alex for the appointment. Because that simple question had crystallized everything he’d been noticing, everything he’d been too afraid to name.

*Does it feel different when you think?*

She genuinely didn’t understand that other people experienced consciousness differently. To her, having two brains, two bodies, and two sets of hands—it was just how thinking felt. How being alive felt.

How being *herself* felt.

\*\*\*

Now, in the present moment, with her four little feet warm in his hands and her dual breathing steady and synchronized, Amerigo understood.

<!-- page -->

The potty training. The synchronized reactions to food. The way she never argued with “herself.” The reclamatic incident. The calculus. All of it.

It wasn’t coincidence or trained behavior or exceptional twin intuition.

It was Celectra being Celectra. One mind. One consciousness. One extraordinary person who happened to have two bodies, the way some people happen to have blue eyes or perfect pitch.

The radiation that had killed Jenni hadn’t destroyed their daughters. It had *transformed* them. Created something new. Something that had never existed before in all of human history.

And Jenni had known. Had felt it even before they were born. *They’re special. They’re connected. Don’t let anyone make them feel wrong for it.*

“Daddy?” Both voices spoke at once, soft with concern. “Are you okay? You’re thinking really loud.”

He smiled, despite the tears threatening to fall, and reached over to brush a strand of white hair from one of her faces. “Just remembering your mommy. And thinking about how proud she’d be of you.”

Both bodies shifted, setting down her holopads, both sets of eyes focusing on him with that unnerving, perfect attention. “Tell me something about her. Something you haven’t told me before.”

He thought for a moment, still absently rubbing her feet, feeling the warmth of her—singular her—spread across the sofa. There were so many memories. So many stories.

“She used to sing in the shower,” he said finally. “Terribly. Off-key. Songs from Earth that nobody else in the system had even heard of. And she’d make up half the words when she forgot them.”

Both faces lit up with delight. “Like what?”

<!-- page -->

“There was this old song called ‘Twinkle Twinkle Little Star’—except she’d sing it ‘Sparkle sparkle little sun, burning bright when day is done.’” He smiled at the memory and could almost hear her voice echoing off the shower walls. “She said the original didn’t make sense because stars burn during the day too; we just can’t see them.”

Both bodies giggled—a sound of pure joy that seemed to fill the room. “That does make more sense\!”

“She always made sense. Even when she was being silly.” He squeezed her feet gently, grounding himself in the physical reality of her. “You’re like her that way. Brilliant and joyful and... unique. In the best possible way.”

A pause. Her bodies had gone still, both sets of eyes distant.

“Daddy?”

“Yes, sweetheart?”

“Do you think she knew? Before she... before she left? That I’d be like this?”

The question hit him like a physical blow. He thought about Jenni’s last words, spoken with such urgency: *They’re special. I felt it. They’re connected somehow.*

“Yeah,” he whispered, voice thick. “I think she knew. I think she felt you—really felt you, the real you—before anyone else did. Long before I did.”

Both bodies moved then, abandoning their positions on the sofa arms to curl up against him, four arms wrapping around his torso, two heads finding the hollows of his shoulders.

“I wish I could have met her,” Celectra said softly. “I wish I could remember her.”

“You do, in a way.” Amerigo held her—held *her*, singular, miraculous her—and let himself finally grieve and celebrate at once. “Every time you figure out a puzzle or ask a brilliant question or refuse to accept easy answers. Every time you see beauty in strange places. That’s all her.”

<!-- page -->

He paused, then added, “And every time you use your uniqueness as a strength instead of hiding it—that’s you honoring what she gave up everything for. You existing, just as you are, is what she wanted most.”

They sat like that for a long time, father and daughter, wrapped in memory and love and the weight of a legacy neither had asked for but both would carry forward.

Outside the porthole in the floor, Ares spun past in shades of rust and shadow—the same moon Jenni had surveyed, had mapped, and had helped transform into humanity’s new frontier.

Somewhere on that moon, in labs and homes and schools, the colony was growing, thriving, and building the world Jenni had dreamed of.

And here, on this station, her daughter—singular, extraordinary, impossible—was growing too.

“I love you, Celectra,” Amerigo whispered into her white hair. “Both of you. All of you. Every part of you.”

“I love you too, Daddy,” she replied, her voices harmonizing perfectly. “All of me loves all of you.”

And for the first time since Jenni’s death, Amerigo felt something like peace.
