The classroom met on Tuesday and Thursday mornings in the Nexus's largest room, which had been reconfigured for the purpose—desks arranged in a horseshoe, a holo-display at the open end, and two chairs at the front that Celectra had positioned herself in, one body in each, before the first child arrived.

Santos had insisted on adult oversight for the sessions, which Celectra considered reasonable. A ten-year-old teaching other children about first contact was—she had heard Santos use the phrase "extraordinarily irregular" twice—something that required a professional framework around it. Zoë served as session lead on record; Lira Keahi, at sixteen, managed the display when Utara wasn't doing it and distributed materials with the organized efficiency of someone who had decided to take this seriously. The arrangement meant Celectra could simply teach, which suited her considerably better than managing a room.

She had been nervous the first week. She wasn't nervous anymore.

There were eleven of them this Thursday, ranging from six to sixteen—a cross-section of New Eden's younger generation that Zoë and Santos had assembled with some care, drawing from families already briefed on first contact and cleared for basic disclosure. It wasn't a secret exactly, not anymore, but it was still managed. The colony's adults were still finding their footing with the knowledge; the children, in Celectra's experience, were considerably faster.

"Last week we talked about how the Galinstanians perceive their environment," she said, one body writing key terms on the display while the other faced the class. "Today I want to start somewhere different. I want to start with something that confused me for a long time." She set down her stylus with her left hand and picked up the celestite specimen from the side table—pale blue-grey, its surface catching the morning light from the east-facing windows. "What is this?"

"A rock," said a boy named Kai Vasquez, age twelve, with the confidence of someone who considers this unanswerable.

<!-- page -->

"A mineral specimen," said Aulii Vongphachanh, age eleven, who considered precision a form of courtesy. Aulii was compact and careful in everything she did, including questions. Her mother sat on the athletics committee, and her father worked on the colony's water reclamation systems. She had been coming to Celectra's sessions since the first week without missing once.

"Both," Celectra agreed. "It's celestite. It came from Oisín, the closest planet to Niamh. My mother found it via a sample collection drone on a survey mission before I was born." She set it down. "Now. Is it art?"

Silence. The particular silence of children deciding whether the question is a trick.

"It's pretty," offered a girl named Maya Keahi, who was nine and almost always had a sketchbook open on her desk, which Celectra had stopped discouraging after the second session because Maya's drawings of what Celectra described had turned out to be the best teaching tool in the room. "Things can be pretty without being art."

"Can they?"

Maya considered this with the seriousness she gave most things. "I think art is when someone chooses to make something. The rock just—happened."

<!-- page -->

"That's a useful distinction," Celectra said. "Hold onto it. Because the Galinstanians have been doing something in the caves for hundreds of thousands of years, and whether it's art or something else entirely is one of the most interesting questions I've come across." She nodded to Utara, who ran the display for her on session days. "Can you put up the scan from the committee visit, please? The fractal wall section."

The holo-display bloomed with Uncle Doc's imaging data—the fractal geometries of the carved granite walls, the bismuth veins threading through them at every scale, the patterns repeating from the macro level down to the resolution limit of the scanner.

The room went quiet in a different way.

"That's underground?" Aulii asked.

"About two kilometers below where we're sitting."

"And they made it."

<!-- page -->

"They metabolized their way through it," Celectra said. "The Galinstanians eat rock—they dissolve the mineral content they need, and the structure left behind is what you're looking at. So the architecture is literally what remains after they've taken their nutrition from the stone." She let that sit. "Now. Is that art?"

"It's both," Maya said immediately, her pencil already moving. "Like how birds build nests—it's practical, but it's also beautiful, and they choose the shape even if the choice is instinctive."

"Stan would find that analogy interesting," Celectra said. "I'll tell him."

"Stan talks to you?" asked Kai.

"Stan thinks to me. That's how it works—they modulate electromagnetic fields, and my brain can receive the signal. You can all receive it too when you're in the caves. You'll feel it as words, roughly, though it's more layered than that. Like hearing music and understanding what it's about even if you don't speak the composer's language."

"But we can't talk back," said Maya’s older sister, Lira Keahi, age sixteen and the oldest in the group, who had the habit of cutting directly to the limiting principle of any situation.

<!-- page -->

"No. Only I can transmit on their frequency. Everyone else is silent to them the way a radio station is silent to other radio stations—you're broadcasting, but not with enough amplitude; the signal strength is too low."

"Why you?" Lira asked. Not unkindly. Just precisely.

"That's actually the most important question in this whole session," Celectra said. "And the honest answer is, because of the same solar flare that makes me what I am."

She had explained this before—to Zoë, to Uncle Doc, to Santos—but not to children, and children asked different questions than adults did. Adults asked about mechanism. Children asked about what it felt like.

She sat with it for a moment, both bodies, finding the right words.

"When I was in my mother's womb," she said, "there was a solar flare. A big one. It exposed her—and me—to a lot of radiation at a very specific moment when my brains were forming. Instead of developing as two separate identities with identical bodies, which was what should have happened—" she paused— "or instead of being damaged beyond survival, which was the more likely outcome, my brains built a connection between them. A bridge. They had to shout to each other across the space between my two heads, and over time the shouting became a signal, and the signal became—me."

<!-- page -->

She touched the celestite with one hand, the other held open on her knee.

"The signal my brains use to talk to each other runs in the extremely low frequency range. The same range the Galinstanians use to think. When I'm near the bismuth network, my ambient processing—just the background noise of my mind doing what minds do—broadcasts on their frequency. They received it the first time I visited New Eden and understood it immediately, the way you'd understand emotion in someone's voice even if you didn't speak their language." Both her mouths curved. "They've told me I felt curious. They said curiosity was the first thing they heard clearly, after seventeen years of noise from our radios that meant nothing to them."

"Were you scared?" Maya asked. She had stopped drawing. "When Stan first talked to you in the caves?"

"Yes," Celectra said, without hesitation. "And curious. Both at once. Which is probably why he recognized me."

Laughter. She had learned, over weeks of these sessions, exactly where the laughter was.

"I have a question," said a boy named Chen Wei, age seven, who had been quiet for most of the session with the concentrated quiet of someone assembling something complicated. "If they can feel emotions through the network, and I'm near the network, can they feel my emotions?"

<!-- page -->

Celectra looked at him. "What makes you ask?"

"Because if they can feel when someone is sad—" he stopped, reformulated— "I mean, if a feeling is like a frequency, and the network carries frequencies, then wouldn't everyone near the network be broadcasting whether they wanted to or not?"

The room was very still.

Celectra looked at Chen Wei with both sets of eyes and felt the particular quality of attention she reserved for questions that deserved it. He was seven years old, and he had just identified something it had taken Roan three cave visits to articulate.

"You're right," she said. "And wrong. Human neural signals don't carry outside the scalp in a way the Galinstanians can receive—your brains don't broadcast on their frequency. You're silent to them, the same way most radio stations are silent to each other. The exception is me." She paused. "But what you've just described—the idea that being near the network means broadcasting whether you intend to or not—that's exactly my situation. Everything I feel and perceive near a bismuth vein reaches them to some degree. Not as words. As texture. Temperature. The quality of a feeling rather than its content."

Chen Wei nodded, satisfied, and went back to his notes.

<!-- page -->

"Which means," Lira said, connecting the thread, "that you have to be careful. Near the network."

"I have to be aware," Celectra corrected gently. "Not careful in the sense of hiding—they've been clear that they don't want me to manage what I send, because managed feelings are harder to read than genuine ones. But to be aware that what I'm experiencing is reaching them. Which means—" she looked around the room— "it's one of many reasons why I don't take everyone down to the caves. The Galinstanians are a discovery, not an experience. Not yet."

"When will they be?" Maya asked.

"When we've had enough time to understand each other properly. When there are protocols in place for how contact happens and who it happens with." She picked up the celestite again. "This rock took a very long time to form. Good things often do."

They broke for fifteen minutes mid-session, the children spilling into the corridor for water and movement. Celectra stayed at the front, one body reviewing notes while the other stretched, and Aulii lingered—she usually did, with questions she'd apparently decided were too specific for the group.

"The fractal wall pattern," Aulii said, setting her notes on the desk between them. "You said it encodes their history. Like a record." She had drawn a diagram—precise, geometric—of what she'd seen on the display. "Is it read-only? Or can they write to it?"

<!-- page -->

"They write to it constantly," Celectra said. "Every significant event, every major decision, every shift in the network's structure. Stan says if you knew how to read it, you could trace the entire history of the species back to the Great Resonance."

"But you can't read it."

"Not yet. Stan's been—" she searched for the right word— "orienting me. Showing me how the patterns relate to each other. It's not a language exactly. More like a topology. The shape of the record matters as much as its content." Both her mouths curved. "I've been studying it for three months, and I think I understand about two percent of it."

Aulii's pencil was moving before Celectra finished speaking. "That's a project," she said.

"That's a lifetime of projects," Celectra agreed. "Probably more than one lifetime."

Aulii looked up. "Could a human learn to read it? With enough time?"

<!-- page -->

"I genuinely don't know. Stan thinks it's possible in principle—the pattern recognition required isn't beyond human cognition, he says. But it would need someone who could spend a lot of time near the network, receiving, before anything started to resolve into meaning." She picked up her stylus. "Which is another reason why the access protocols matter. Comprehension isn't something you can rush."

Aulii absorbed this with the same expression she'd had at the karate tournament—not frustrated by a limitation, but calculating the path around it. Celectra had noticed this about her in the first session and had been tracking it since. Aulii Vongphachanh did not accept walls as final answers. She looked for the door.

"I want to learn," Aulii said. It wasn't a request exactly. It was closer to a statement of intent.

"I know," Celectra said. "I'll tell Stan."

The afternoon session ran long because Maya had brought her sketchbook, and once she started sharing it—Galinstanians flowing along the cave floor in careful anatomically-plausible detail, the bismuth vein cross-sections rendered with an accuracy that made Celectra stop and stare, a diagram of the ELF frequency spectrum with human neural range and Galinstanian range shown in overlapping bands—the class had dissolved into something more like a seminar, everyone leaning over the book and asking questions that generated other questions.

<!-- page -->

"How did you know the field lines would curve like that?" Celectra asked, pointing at Maya's rendering of a Galinstanian in motion, the electromagnetic field around its form shown as visible arcs.

"I asked Utara for a model of galinstan conductivity and electromagnetic field propagation and then I drew what that would look like if it were also alive," Maya said, as if this were obvious. "Is it wrong?"

"It's probably right," Celectra said slowly. "Dr. Cruiz's scanner readings suggest the field shape during active transmission looks very close to this." She looked at the girl with both sets of eyes. "How long did this take you?"

"A few evenings."

"Have you shown this to Uncle—to Dr. Cruiz?"

Maya shook her head.

<!-- page -->

"He would very much like to see it," Celectra said. "Will you let me show him?"

The flush of pleasure across Maya's face was immediate and total, the kind that arrives before a person can decide whether to show it. "Yes," she said. "I mean—yes. If it's actually useful."

"It's the clearest visual representation of Galinstanian field dynamics I've seen," Celectra said. "It's useful."

She added it to her notes and moved back to the front of the room, and the session continued.

⁂

When the last child had gone and the room was quiet, Celectra gathered her materials with the methodical efficiency of someone who had developed a post-session routine: notes sorted, display cleared, the celestite back in the specimen bag, the two chairs returned to their positions facing each other rather than the class. She had started sitting in both at the end of each session—a small private ritual, the two bodies facing each other in the empty room, the particular comfort of being fully present in her own company for a moment before the day continued.

<!-- page -->

She sat.

Through the east-facing windows, the afternoon light was changing, the twin stars beginning their descent toward the ridge. New Eden's sounds drifted up from the plaza below.

She thought about Chen Wei's question. About frequencies and broadcasting and the difference between sending and receiving. She thought about Aulii's careful notebook and Maya's field-line drawings and the way Lira had cut directly to the limiting principle, and she thought that the colony's next generation was going to be considerably less afraid of the Galinstanians than its first one had been, and that this was largely because of what was happening in this room on Tuesday and Thursday mornings, and that was—significant.

She sat with that for a moment, both bodies, both sets of eyes on the door the children had walked out of.

It was one thing to be a bridge. Bridges were useful, but they didn't move—they waited for people to decide to cross. What she was building in this room was something different. She was building people who would want to.
