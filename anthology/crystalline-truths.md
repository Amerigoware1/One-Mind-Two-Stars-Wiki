Title: Cyrstalling Truths
---

​Celectra woke in the pre-dawn dimness. One body rolled from bed, knees sinking into the soft-touch flooring. The other leaned over the edge, fingers finding the carbon fiber panel set into the bedframe. A near-silent *whir* of electric motors followed, and a powered drawer, faced with matching cherry veneer, eased itself open from the decking until it was fully extended. It hung suspended just over the floor.

​Celectra’s kneeling body leaned in to watch the magic. The top surface didn’t slide or lift—instead, the two halves of the drawer’s lid *opened up and away* from the center on synchronized hydraulic arms, like the wings of a massive insect.  

​This wasn’t just a drawer; it was a powered field case. As the lids opened, they revealed three-tiered layers of organized compartments, folding out like a jeweler’s display. The lowest section—a velvet-lined, deep well—held her testing tools in the drawer casing, but the three upper tiers of specimens were now cantilevered out, glowing faintly in the dimness.  

​Inside, specimens lined up in neat rows—each one labeled in her careful handwriting with names she’d learned from Daddy’s stories and Utara’s lessons. Agate, iron pyrite, and raw amethyst sat on the top tiers, all bathed in the faint, low-light illumination built into the case’s mechanism. The smell of cold rock and faint machine oil filled the air. Her fingers knew the compartments by touch.  

The bismuth ore from yesterday called to her. She lifted it out, turning it under her magnifying lamp. The iridescent sheen danced through pink and green and purple, and she felt that familiar tingle of curiosity in her mind.  

“Hardness test first,” she whispered to herself. She liked talking through her experiments, even when no one was listening. It helped her think.  

One body held the bismuth steady while the other selected the quartz crystal from her collection. She drew the quartz across the bismuth’s surface, applying gentle pressure. The bismuth yielded, leaving a faint scratch. “Softer than quartz,” she murmured, making a note on her holopad. “Probably Mohs three or four.”  

Next, the magnet test. She brought the small neodymium magnet close and felt a tiny resistance, like the bismuth was pushing back ever so slightly. “Weird.” She tried it twice more to be sure. “It’s not magnetic, but it’s not nothing either. Diamagnetic?” She’d read about that in one of her books. Most things were either pulled toward magnets or ignored them, but a few special materials pushed back just a little.  

She pulled out her dropper bottle of vinegar—Daddy had shown her how acids reacted with some minerals but not others. A single tiny drop on one corner of the sample. She waited, watching intently with both sets of eyes from different angles, one profile, the other overhead, getting a fuller view of the specimen than anyone with just one body could manage.  
Nothing happened. The vinegar just sat there, not fizzing or bubbling or doing anything interesting.  

“Non-reactive with weak acids,” she said, dabbing it away with a cloth. She made another note.  
She held the bismuth under the magnifying lamp, one body sketching its crystalline structure while the other studied the rainbow sheen. “The colors are from light interference,” she said, remembering what Utara had taught her about thin films. “The oxide layer must be really, really thin. Just a few hundred nanometers, maybe?”  

She loved words like nanometers. They made her feel like a real scientist.  

Carefully, she placed the bismuth in an empty slot in her collection. She pulled out a blank label card and filled it in with her neatest handwriting:  
Specimen \#47 \- Bismuth Ore  
Pison River Cave System  
Collected birthday seven, Earth standard  
First cave specimen  

Her pen hovered over the card. She thought about adding more—about how Daddy had found it, about the mysterious tunnel that went deeper—but the case was getting full and the cards were small.

Instead, she whispered, “I bet you would’ve liked this one.”  

She wasn’t even sure who she was talking to. The rocks? The collection itself? Or...  

“Daddy says you could read a whole planet’s history from its rocks,” she continued softly, one finger tracing the edge of the card. “I’m trying to learn how. I want to...”  

Want to what? Be like a mother she’d never met? Understand the woman in Daddy’s stories and the framed photo on his wall? Sometimes she felt like she should miss her mommy more, but how could you miss someone you’d never known?  

She closed the case with a soft click, the moment feeling too big and confusing for pre-dawn darkness.  

“Daddy\!” She startled when she looked up and saw him in the doorway. A flush warmed her cheeks. “I didn’t hear you.”  

“I woke up early. It looks like we both did,” he said, stepping into the room. His dark chocolate eyes—so much like her own milk chocolate ones—looked soft in the lamplight. “Couldn’t help but watch. That was impressive, sweetheart. Really impressive.”  

“I was just testing the birthday rock,” she said, suddenly feeling defensive and proud at the same time. Why was she embarrassed? She hadn’t done anything wrong. “I wanted to do it right. Scientific method and everything.”  

“Where did you learn about diamagnetism? And nanometers?”  

One shoulder lifted in a shrug as her other body rose. “Utara teaches me stuff when I ask. And I read a lot. You know that.”  

He knelt beside her, and she could see something in his expression—that look he got sometimes, like he was trying to figure out a really hard equation. “Your mother would be amazed by you. The way you tested that sample—that’s exactly how she worked. Methodical. Curious. Never satisfied with just one test.”  

“Really?” Both sets of eyes brightened. She loved hearing about her mommy, even if it felt like learning about a character in a story rather than a real person.  

“Really. She’d spend hours analyzing core samples, running test after test, always asking, “What does this tell me about how it formed?”” He touched the cheek of each cherubic face gently, and she leaned into his hands. “You’ve got her scientific mind. And my engineering brain. That’s a powerful combination.”  

Celectra beamed. She threw all four arms around him in a double hug, squeezing tight. “Thanks, Daddy.”  

“Now,” he said, extricating himself with a chuckle, “how about we get some breakfast before your appointment with Uncle Doc?”  

Her enthusiasm dimmed. She pulled back, both bodies settling onto her heels. “Do I have to go? I feel fine.”  

“I know you do. But Uncle Doc might be able to help us understand...” He paused, and she saw that look again. The one that meant he was trying to figure her out. “Help us understand why you can feel things in both your bodies at the same time. Why you’re... the way you are.”  

*Why I’m one person and you still can’t quite believe it,* she thought, but didn’t say out loud. He was trying. She could see he was trying really hard.  

She tilted her heads slightly, considering. *Maybe Uncle Doc would finally make Daddy understand. Maybe he’d have the right words to explain what she’d been trying to tell him for years.*  

“Okay,” she said. “But can I bring one of my specimens to show him? I want him to know I’m not just good at math.”  

“I think that’s a great idea.”  

She pulled the case back out and studied her collection. The celestine from Oisín caught her eye—a beautiful pale-blue-gray crystal. Celestine. Celectra. She'd noticed the similarity years ago. As if the universe had left a clue in the rocks themselves. She lifted it carefully. “This one,” she decided. “It’s pretty, and it has a good story.”  

As she stood, clutching the celestite specimen in one hand while her other body reached for Daddy’s hand, she felt that familiar sensation—the way her two bodies moved together like breathing, as natural as thinking, and as much a part of her as her own heartbeat.  

Maybe today Uncle Doc would finally help Daddy see what she’d always known: that she wasn’t Celeste and Electra. She was just Celectra. one person whose hands moved as one thought—no translation needed between them.  

“Ready?” Daddy asked.  

“Ready,” she replied.  

Together, they headed to the kitchen, the station’s lighting sequence finally beginning its gradual brightening as Utopis turned its face toward another day.
