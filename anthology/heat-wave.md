The heatwave struck in mid-May with the particular brutality that came when Ares's slow periastron approach coincided with the binary pair's mutual alignment. Niamh and Ciara, usually courteous in their dance, occasionally synchronized their maximums. When their peaks overlapped, the combined radiative flux became punishing.

The colony's climate control systems strained under the demand. Buildings designed for moderate temperature regulation struggled to compensate for forty-degree external heat. Rolling brownouts affected nonessential systems as power diverted to cooling.

The twins were miserable. Mara's normally placid temperament crumbled into fussy, flushed irritability. Jenni, never calm in the best circumstances, became a tiny engine of distress.

Celectra's right body held Mara at 1400, the baby hot against her skin despite minimal clothing. Her left body managed Jenni, who was crying with the particular intensity of a child who couldn't articulate what was wrong but knew everything was terrible.

The nursery was stifling. Celectra wore her Phase One headset and thought, with desperate clarity: Too hot. Need cooler. Please.

"Cooling request acknowledged," Utara replied. "However, system capacity is currently limited due to colony-wide demand. Adjusting to minimum achievable temperature: twenty-six degrees. This is three degrees above your preset preference."

<!-- page -->

Twenty-six was better than twenty-nine, but both babies were still flushed, still fussing. Celectra tried ice packs, cool baths, minimal clothing. Nothing provided more than temporary relief.

At 1430, she realized Mara's temperature was elevated. Not drastically, but enough to concern her. She set the baby down long enough to check with a medical scanner: 38.2 degrees Celsius.

Fear spiked through her consciousness—both bodies' hearts racing.

She'd been wearing the headset for an hour. The ANI had been reading her biomarkers that entire time: skin temperature elevated from holding hot baby, heart rate increasing with stress, neural patterns showing rising anxiety.

But she'd been focused on Jenni. On managing. On trying to keep both twins comfortable. She hadn't consciously thought to request additional cooling until Mara was already showing fever-range temperature.

Uncle Alex arrived within minutes of her comm. He assessed Mara with practiced efficiency: "She's okay. Just overheated. Cool bath, fluids, monitor her. But she's not in danger."

<!-- page -->

The crisis passed. Both twins were given lukewarm baths, put in diapers only, placed in the coolest room of the house with portable cooling units Jaxon brought from the facility. By 1700, both babies were comfortable again, drowsing in their portable cribs while the heatwave continued raging outside.

Celectra sat between the cribs—both bodies on the floor, backs against the wall—and felt the realization crystallize:

The ANI had known. It had been reading her biomarkers, tracking her rising stress, monitoring the environmental data. It knew Mara was getting too hot before Celectra had consciously registered the danger.

But it couldn't act on that knowledge. Because she hadn't given it permission.

Phase One was reactive. She had to notice the problem, formulate the intent, think the command.

But what if the system could be proactive? What if it could listen not just to her conscious commands, but to her body's distress signals?

<!-- page -->

What if Utara could care for her the way she tried to care for her daughters—noticing needs before they became crises?

\*\*\*

That evening, after the twins were finally asleep and the temperature had dropped to bearable levels, Celectra and Jaxon sat in their living room addressing Utara's interface.

"Could you accept a continuous biometric stream from ANI?" Celectra asked, both voices careful. "Not just waiting for thought-commands, but monitoring stress, discomfort, fatigue constantly?"

"Yes," Utara replied without hesitation. "ANI devices already monitor heart rate, skin temperature, stress indicators, and emotional state for communion clarity optimization. I could receive this data stream and respond to detected needs within user-defined parameters."

"You could detect when someone's overheating before they consciously notice?" Jaxon asked.

<!-- page -->

"Correct. I could also detect fatigue, stress, discomfort, and other states that impair well-being. However, I would require explicit consent for this level of monitoring and clear guidelines for autonomous response. This is significantly more intimate than command interface."

Celectra's four hemispheres processed the implications simultaneously:

Left-analytical: *Continuous monitoring enables proactive intervention. Prevents crises rather than responding to them. Significant improvement in care capability.*

Right-analytical: *But requires users to accept constant observation. Privacy concerns escalate. Not everyone will be comfortable with this level of transparency.*

Left-emotional: *Today Mara got too hot because I was focused elsewhere. This could have prevented that. Could prevent worse.*

Right-emotional: *But do I want to be watched constantly? Even by an AI I trust? The vulnerability feels enormous.*

<!-- page -->

"What if we designed it as opt-in?" her right voice asked slowly. "Users choose whether to enable adaptive monitoring. Set their own comfort ranges. Can disable it anytime?"

"That would be acceptable," Utara confirmed. "I would also recommend transparency protocols. Users should be able to see what I detect and why I respond as I do. Visibility builds trust."

"Show us what that would look like," both voices requested.

Maya's visual interface appeared on the main display—a mockup showing real-time biometric data. Heart rate, skin temperature, breathing pattern, stress indicators, all displayed cleanly with clear explanations.

Sample scenario: "Heart rate elevated (85 bpm), skin temperature rising (37.1°C) → cooling room by 2°C within your preset range (20-23°C). Rationale: physiological stress detected, proactive comfort intervention authorized."

"Users see this?" Jaxon asked.

<!-- page -->

"If they want to," Maya confirmed. "Can be displayed on any screen, or just background monitoring with audio confirmation."

Sam's audio cue played—a soft, three-tone chime. "Gentle enough not to startle, distinct enough to register. Indicates Utara taking adaptive action."

The system wasn't invisible. It was transparent. Observable. Auditable.

"I want to test it," both voices said. "In the nursery first. With the twins. If it works there, in the most chaotic environment possible, it'll work anywhere."

*Celectra's first published story, "The Shade-Beast of Golden Reeds," appeared in the Utopis Literary Review when she was fourteen. Critics called it "a child's allegory for courage." Only Zoë recognized the boar's huffing as Dr. Kret's voice—and the barycenter metaphor as Celectra's quiet manifesto: "Not two stars. Just one dance."*
