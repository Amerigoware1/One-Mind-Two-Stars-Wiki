Amerigo jolted awake to the sound of a pint-sized invasion force storming his berth aboard the space station, Utopis. His twin daughters, a whirlwind of boundless energy, quickly flanked his bed, seizing his arms and launching an adorable onslaught of demands, their voices a synchronized clamor in his room. This wasn’t the first time this had happened, and he knew it wouldn’t be the last. Despite his lean and muscular physique, he was utterly powerless against their formidable attributes of charm and puppy-dog eyes.  

The soft blue ambient lighting strips along the curved ceiling had already begun their gradual morning brightening sequence, synced with Ares’s surface below, where dawn was still an hour away.  

Squinting at his bedside chronometer, magnetically secured to the textured gray wall panel next to a framed photo of Jenni, he saw it was only 5:12. A groan escaped his lips as he muttered, “Coffee.” The mini molecular reconfiguration engine on his bedside table whirred to life, synthesizing a steaming cup of coffee. Its robust aroma wafted through the recycled air, cutting through the station’s ever-present metallic tang.  

He freed his right arm and sat up, reaching for the tantalizing brew. As he took a few sips, the cobwebs in his mind dissipated. His attackers’ insistent demands cut through the morning haze. 

“Come on, Daddy, get up\!” one of them pleaded. “I want to go to the surface. Are you going to sleep all day? It’s my birthday\! Come on, Daddy\!”  

Taking a couple more cautious sips, Amerigo addressed his bouncing offspring on his right side with long white hair. “What makes you want to go to that boring, desolate place, Celeste?” he asked, his gaze confirming her identity with her star-shaped gold earrings.  

“I want to go swimming\!” Celeste exclaimed, her voice brimming with excitement.  

Amerigo turned to the identical, milk-chocolate-eyed, pale figure tugging on his left arm. “And what do you want to do, Electra?” he inquired, his tone equally affectionate.  

“I just said, ‘I want to go swimming\!’” Electra replied, her voice laced with a hint of exasperation.  

“No, you didn’t,” Amerigo countered. “Your sister did.”  

They retorted stereophonically, “For the zillionth time, I am only one person, Daddy\!”  

Amerigo sighed, familiar with this refrain. “Whatever you say, kids. Go swim in the hot tub.”  

“It’s too small\! I want to go swimming in the river\!” they protested in unison.  

Amerigo grinned, his dark chocolate eyes twinkling with amusement and warmth. “Well, I’ll think about it,” he promised. “Now, run along and put some clothes on so I can get up.”  

With a playful flurry of raspberry blows to their father’s cheeks, his pride and joy scampered out of his bedroom, their bare feet silent on the soft-touch flooring that covered their quarter’s metal decking.  

Amerigo sleepily donned swim trunks in place of boxers, then shrugged into the bright, white jumpsuit that was the hallmark of his profession as a scientist. With a final yawn and a glance at the chronometer—still too early by any reasonable standard—he headed for the bathroom.  

A few minutes later, Amerigo entered the open-plan living area that served as their kitchen, dining, and living space. The modular dining table had extended from the wall, and his daughters sat at it, their plates already filled with breakfast. "In the middle of the room, a circular porthole a meter across gleamed underfoot, its layered quartz-sapphire surface clear enough to show the rusty-hued moon Ares gliding past far below—its cratered face illuminated by the distant fire of Niamh and Ciara, while the colossal swirls of its parent planet Chaos dominated the sky beyond."  

“Happy birthday, kids\!” he exclaimed, ignoring that they had not returned to their bedroom to dress. He leaned down and kissed each of their toast-and-jelly-smudged lips, then ruffled both heads of white hair. “Happy birthday to my brown-eyed girls,” he said warmly, the phrase as natural as breathing. He’d been calling them that since they were born—those milk chocolate eyes lighter than his own dark chocolate brown, inherited from him rather than Jenni’s hazel.  

“So, how old are you today, twenty-one in Earth standard?”  

“Nope, Daddy\!” Celeste replied, her eyes sparkling with mischief. “Just one-third of that\!” 

“Seven Earth years,” Electra added proudly from the other side, “but on my other birthday, I’ll turn seven again, but that isn’t until Ares makes six and a half more trips around Chaos.”  

Amerigo chuckled, his heart overflowing with affection for his daughters. The dual calendar system still amused him—seven years by Earth reckoning, but only six and a quarter local years, with her Ares birthday still six months away in Ares time.  

Turning to the larger molecular reconfiguration engine built seamlessly into the kitchen wall, he instructed, “Multigrain hot cereal, a banana, and toast, please.” Within seconds, a steaming bowl of cereal, a perfectly yellow banana, and two crisp toast slices materialized, their nutty aroma filling the air.  

Between mouthfuls of cereal, the girls once again discussed their desired birthday activity. While fully intending to grant their wish, Amerigo playfully argued against it. “But it’s dusty, hot, and dry down there,” he countered.  

“Not at the river\!” Electra insisted, her eyes sparkling with determination.  

“And what about your skin sensitivity?” Amerigo pressed, feigning concern. “You’ll burn up in all that sunlight.”  

Celeste’s expression remained undeterred. “There should be plenty of shade there, and I’ve got my skin cream and sun hats,” she assured him.  

Amerigo shifted tactics. “Well, what about your education?” he asked, raising an eyebrow.  

“Education? Surely one day won’t hurt,” Celeste countered, her voice laced with mock indignation. “It’s my birthday, after all\! Besides, how many kids my age know that seven is one-third of twenty-one? And I already gave Utara the day off.”  

She continued, “I’ve completed 147% of the standard curriculum for my age cohort, Daddy. A single day’s deviation represents a 0.27% reduction in annual progress—statistically negligible. Besides, Utara agreed my request was ‘logically sound.’”  

The mental image of his daughters politely informing the Unified Thought and Resource Architecture—the same AI that managed interplanetary communications and resource allocation—that her teaching services would not be required today struck Amerigo as wonderfully absurd.  

The sentient AI, Utara, had long since accepted being treated as one of them, even adjusting her voice when the colonists started calling her “she.” It had seemed like such a small change at the time. Now, it was hard to imagine the colony without her.  

He burst into laughter. “None that I can think of,” he admitted. “Well, finish your breakfast, and then we’ll see.”  

When they finished their meal, Amerigo diligently stacked the used dishes on the MRE’s base plate. The machine hummed to life, dissolving the objects into their molecular components, feeding into the station’s main matter reclamation system.

With a light skip in her step, Celectra—her name, her truth, the word she'd carved from their mistaken labels at age five—headed toward her berth. Her compact bedroom mirrored her daddy’s layout but carried her own touch—constellation charts pinned magnetically to the walls and a handful of physical books tucked into the shelving. She felt sure Daddy’s teasing had hidden the truth: he meant to grant her wish. She had always read him well, catching the twinkle in his eyes and the smile that never quite reached his lips.  

In the sanctuary of her room, Celectra smoothed a protective cream onto her ultra-sensitive, flare-marked skin, the ritual as much armor as comfort, renewing the thin barrier that kept her fragile, flare-marked skin from rebelling. Beneath her excitement for the day, a deeper resolve stirred. Today would be the day she made him truly see her—not Celeste and Electra, but Celectra, whole.  

One body padded to the bathroom while her other went to her closet, stretching. She’d learned early that morning routines worked better split—why wait for one body to finish brushing teeth when the other could be picking out clothes?  

In the bathroom, she took her vitamins and then squeezed paste onto her toothbrush, the minty smell sharp in the recycled air. Her other body, meanwhile, opened the closet and ran fingers along the hanging fabrics. cyro-silk”, a graphene-infused high-denier lyocell material. Always cryo-silk. The synthetic blends the MRE could produce left her skin raw and itching within hours.  

Brushing teeth with one body, she selected a simple blue dress with her other. Then, because Daddy liked it when she “matched,” she grabbed an identical one in green for her other body. Not matching-matching—she wasn’t twins, no matter what people thought—but complementary. Two halves of a whole.  

Toothbrushing finished, she rinsed, then swapped tasks. Her blue-dressed body headed to the bathroom to brush its teeth while her other body slipped the green dress over her head. The fabric whispered against her pale skin, comfortable and familiar.  

By the time her blue-dressed body finished in the bathroom, her green one was already brushing its long white hair. They met in the middle of the room, and she worked on braiding one head at a time while the other read, her attention split but whole, like listening to a song in stereo. 

She then slipped her cream into her purse and, using a quick dual parallax view rather than a mirror, confirmed her appearance was flawless, smiling in quiet approval. With her handbag, holopad, and two pairs of sandals waiting by the front door, she was ready—the holopad promising company for the journey ahead.

A short time later, Celectra pressed close to the shuttle’s curved porthole, her breath misting faintly on the thick, layered glass. Outside, Chaos unfurled its fiery enormity. The warm super-Jupiter dominated everything—an ocean of seething color that seemed alive in its own right. Great currents shifted like the muscles of some titanic beast, dark rivers of black and crimson pulling against molten streams of orange and gold. Now and then, a plume erupted from deep within its storms, a burst of incandescent fire that traced the edge of the planet in sudden brilliance before vanishing back into the roiling mass.  

Celectra could almost imagine that the planet was speaking, every flare a gesture, every swirl of color a word too vast for humans to understand. She rested one body’s cheek against the glass while the other hugged her holopad, comforted by the low, steady vibration of the shuttle’s engines. The faint hum threaded through her bones, steady and familiar, like the pulse of home.

To the left, Ares drifted into view—reddish and rugged, its rocky face catching the glow of its monstrous parent. In the moments when it crossed Chaos’s light, its shadow carved a dusky veil across the warm super-Jupiter, as if daring to hide the fury behind it. Celectra loved that sight most of all. A small world standing before a giant, reminding her of the way she always felt between her two selves. Not Celeste, not Electra, but Celectra—whole, if only anyone could see her that way.  

Her eyes lingered on the eclipse, but her mind had already slipped elsewhere. The holopad glowed with a page of text, words weaving pictures in her imagination. Both sets of lips moved faintly as she read, and then, with a sudden spark of inspiration, she turned to Daddy, seated between her two bodies.  

“Daddy,” she asked, her voice colored with both mischief and earnestness, “what is a centaur?”  

Daddy looked up, caught off guard. His brow wrinkled. “Why do you ask, Electra?”  

“Because it’s in this book I’m reading. Right here.”  

His gaze dropped to her hands—empty. More confusion. “What book?”  

Celectra pointed across his lap toward the other side. “No, over there. On your right. He turned, and sure enough, her other self was holding the holopad, one finger pressed against the word she was pretending not to know.  

“So, what is it?” she asked again, from his left.  

Daddy blinked, trying to find the right words. “Well… It’s a creature from mythology. Half horse, half man. Imagine a horse’s body, but instead of a horse’s head, it has the torso of a human rising from its shoulders.” He tilted his head, frowning more deeply. “But how did you know what Celeste was reading if you were staring out the porthole?”  

Both faces turned toward him at once, identical brows lifting in weary exasperation. “Because I’m me, Daddy\!” they chorused.  

Daddy’s expression shifted—his mouth tried for a smile, but his eyes gave him away. He laid a hand gently on her shoulder. “I’m trying to believe you, sweetie. I just want to be sure you’re healthy and happy. Maybe Dr. Cruiz can help us understand better. When we’re back on Utopis, I’ll make an appointment.”  

Celectra leaned into his hand, feeling the warmth of his touch. She nodded, not out of fear but out of curiosity. If anyone could explain the truth of her existence, maybe it was Uncle Doc. Still, part of her wished she could make her father see without anyone else’s help—see her the way she saw herself, as one.

Once they reached the ground at the spaceport, Daddy led her to an autocab. Celectra pressed her nose to the window as they left New Eden behind. The whole village looked strange—houses of stone, steel, and glass clumped around the Plenium, the old colony modules tucked between them like puzzle pieces. Only two hundred adults lived there, and sometimes it felt too crowded, too small.  

The autocab hummed northward, gliding just above the ground. On her right, the Pison River glittered like a ribbon of melted glass, its skinny green banks stitched with GMO trees and bushes that seemed far too fragile for this world. Beyond them stretched endless rust-red plains, cracked and dry like an old clay pot left in the sun. The air shimmered, thirty degrees at least, warm enough that the thought of cool water made her legs twitch.  

To the west, far beyond the settlement’s edge, the fusion reactor’s containment dome caught the morning light—a perfect white sphere sitting alone on the plain like a geometric moon fallen to the ground.   

She caught her breath when Chaos heaved into view in the east. Rising like a living mountain, it glowed crimson in the late morning, so enormous that even the twin stars looked like baubles beside it. Celectra grinned up at its vastness. It didn’t make her feel small—it made her feel alive, part of something great.  

When a perfect spot appeared between two GMO apple trees, Celectra buzzed throughout both of her bodies. “Stop\! Reverse a bit\!” she squealed, pointing. “Look, Daddy\! A beach, and the water looks deep enough to dive\!”  

The moment her sandals hit the sand, joy carried her. The apple trees’ waxy leaves glimmered as if they were made of metal, the grass felt springy under her toes, and the river itself sparkled so clearly she could see pebbles glittering at the bottom. She gasped. “This is fantastic\! Can we stay here forever? It’s so splendiferous\!”  

Daddy chuckled, shaking his head. “Four hours, Baby Girl. That’s all.”  

She barely heard him. The river called louder. The water’s cool scent brushed her face, promising adventure. With a wild whoop, she kicked off her flip-flops, flung her dresses aside, and dove headfirst into the pool.  

The coolness wrapped around her like silk. She surfaced, laughing, wiping water from her faces. From the bank, Daddy tested the water with his toes, patient as always. Shadows stretched behind him—one crisp, one blurry—and Celectra thought they looked like the stars themselves, two lights always overlapping.  

She paddled toward the bank, a sly grin curling her lips. “Daddy, help me out\!” she called, reaching.  

He raised a brow. “Why don’t you come over here and walk up the beach?”  

“But I’m already here,” she said, pretending innocence, though her eyes sparkled with mischief. “Please?”  

He sighed but walked closer. The second his hands met hers, she gave a sharp tug. His feet slipped, and with a splash and shout, Daddy tumbled in beside her. 

Celectra shrieked with laughter, the river ringing with her delight. For two hours, they splashed, dove, chased, and tumbled in the water until the whole pool seemed alive with joy. In that moment, Celectra forgot everything else. There was only sunlight, water, and Daddy’s laughter echoing hers.

Growing tired, Amerigo went ashore all too soon, ignoring his daughters’ playful pleas to stay in the cool water. He chose a flat, grassy area away from the sandy riverbank and used his portable MRE to create a picnic blanket. The air was warm, thick with the scent of river water and the faint sweetness of fruited trees. He moved to a nearby tree, genetically modified to withstand the frequent temperature and lighting changes on Ares, its branches heavy with ripe, crimson apples, and selected two. As he made himself comfortable, he ordered a light lunch—two peanut butter and jelly sandwiches on a tray, along with a knife. With practiced hands, he sliced the apples into thin wedges, the sweet scent of their flesh mingling with the aroma of the sandwiches.  

He glanced at his daughters, still in the water. He could almost feel their reluctance to leave, but he knew better than to argue with their growing hunger. “Lunch\!” he called out, his voice carrying easily over the soft ripple of the small river. They reluctantly emerged, their porcelain skin shimmering under the brilliant, burnt umber-colored light filtered through Ares's iron-rich atmosphere—Niamh's yellow-white glare and Ciara's dim orange glow blending into a single rusty wash.  

Amerigo sighed as he watched them dance across the short, sandy distance, their four dainty feet kicking up puffs of sand. As they passed through an unshaded patch, the starlight seemed to illuminate them from within. Amerigo, feigning a sudden loss of vision, dramatically closed and covered his eyes. “Oh, the light\!” he lamented. “It’s blinding\! A father’s misery\!” He was surprised a second later with twenty little fingers converging in a playful tickle attack that sent Amerigo into fits of laughter.  

Once he had regained his composure, he reminded them to put on their sun hats and generously divided the remaining sandwich and apple, offering each girl a half. They devoured their lunch with gusto, their animated conversation punctuated by playful prodding and teasing—always directed at him, he noticed.  

Amerigo observed, not for the first time, what he perceived as a peculiar dynamic between his daughters. They interacted freely and affectionately with him but never directly with each other. It was a detail he had always dismissed, but today, with the blended light of the binary pair on their faces, it seemed sharper, more significant.  

After satisfying their hunger, the twins meticulously applied a fresh coat of skin cream, their movements synchronized and uncannily alike. Then, they switched gears, applying the cream to each other’s backs without a word or even a glance between them. Celeste paused to scratch an itch on Electra’s shoulder blade, the motion fluid and unthinking, as if guided by an invisible thread rather than sight or word. Amerigo’s breath hitched. A flicker of wonder, tinged with a deep unease, stirred in his chest. How could they move as one without even looking at each other? He couldn’t shake the thought that something was happening just beyond his comprehension.  

His heart nudged him to test the impossible again. Amerigo reached out and gently tickled Electra’s foot, his touch light but deliberate. He watched, mesmerized, as both girls erupted into identical peals of laughter. His pulse quickened—not just from curiosity, but from a deep, aching marvel at the mystery of his daughters. He needed confirmation, something solid to grasp. “Celeste, did you honestly feel that?” he asked, his voice soft, threaded with awe and a trace of apprehension, as if touching the edge of something vast and unknowable.  

“Yes, it tickled\!” Celeste exclaimed, her eyes bright with delight as she pointed to the exact spot on Electra’s foot where Amerigo’s fingers had brushed.  

Amerigo leaned back on the picnic blanket, the soft grass cool against his back. The scientist’s mind in him was racing, building hypotheses and discarding them just as quickly. But his father’s heart felt heavy still. He closed his eyes, the dual laughter echoing in his ears, and let the weight of their connection settle over him. It was impossible, wasn’t it? When he tickled Electra’s foot, Celeste had felt it instantly—not heard, not seen, but *felt*, as if their minds were woven together by some invisible connection. He was a scientist, a man who believed in cause and effect, yet his daughters were defying every known law of biology and physics.  

The twins didn’t let him linger in his thoughts for long. “Come on, Daddy\!” they chimed in unison, their voices bubbling with mischief as they tugged at his hands, one on each wrist. Their boundless energy surged back, their earlier seriousness dissolving into the bright, carefree joy of their birthday outing. As they pulled him toward the river, their bare feet kicking up sand, their laughter blended with the soft ripple of the water against the bank. Amerigo let himself be swept along, his worries momentarily eclipsed by their infectious delight. Still, a part of him turned over the mystery of their connection, like a puzzle he couldn’t yet solve.  

Amerigo’s gaze drifted toward the vertical cliff across the river as his daughters continued their splashy game. The rock face was a dark, striated gray, slick with the constant flow of water. About half a meter below the river’s calm surface, a sizable dark spot caught his eye. It could be a shallow aperture—a small depression in the stone—or a cave.  

His spelunking interests, a childhood fascination he hadn’t thought about in years, eventually won him over. Cautioning the twins to stay out of the water, he swam across the calm channel, the water cool and silky against his skin. Holding his breath, he sank beneath the surface to look closer. Using his hands to guide him down the cliff wall, he lowered himself to the level of the hole. It was slightly more than a meter in diameter and of indeterminable depth, a dark maw in the rock.  

He broke the surface and swam back to the shore, his heart thrumming with a quiet excitement. His daughters, their faces alight with anticipation, eagerly awaited him. “Get my MRE from the blanket, Sweeties,” he requested, and they promptly scampered across the sand and grass to retrieve it as he exited the water.  

With the device in hand, he ordered it to provide him with diving gear equipped with artificial gills and lights. The soft hum of the MRE was a familiar comfort as it materialized the specialized equipment—a black, form-fitting wetsuit and a small helmet with a glowing lamp on the front. He placed the MRE on the riverbank and donned the gear. To test the artificial gills, he re-entered the river and submerged himself briefly, the world below the surface turning silent and muffled. He resurfaced, activated the lights, and watched as a brilliant white beam pierced the translucent water. Satisfied with the equipment’s functionality, he swam back across the narrow river and cautiously ventured into the cave’s depths.  

Amerigo pushed through the narrow opening and found himself in a small chamber. The ceiling rose above the water, its surface rough and uneven. His head broke the surface, and the scent of damp rock and minerals filled his lungs. Stubby stalactites, the color of wet stone, adorned the ceiling, their shapes sculpted by the patient dripping of water over millennia. While not as grand as the fantastical caverns he had imagined, this hidden space had an undeniable sense of age and mystery. It felt like a secret whispered by the planet itself.  

As he swept his headlamp across the walls, a faint, metallic glint caught his eye within a narrow fissure—a jagged seam of dull, silvery material laced with an iridescent sheen, shimmering with faint hues of pink and green. Bismuth ore, Amerigo thought, his mind leaping to its unique crystalline structure and the beautiful oxide layer that gave it that rainbow-like glow. It was a perfect sample to show his girls later, a chance to spark their curiosity about material properties. He marked the spot in his mind.  

A narrow tunnel snaked downward to his right, far too tight for his body to pass. Peering into it, he saw a silvery shimmer, but it vanished when he tried to focus on it. Shrugging, his scientist’s mind dismissed it as water refraction or mineral reflection.  
Small fissures dotted the walls, leaching a ceaseless flow of water that trickled down the rock face. With his curiosity piqued but time pressing, Amerigo exited the cave and swam back across the small river.  

He surfaced directly in front of the girls, their faces alight with anticipation. They were on the riverbank, their small bodies silhouetted against the last of the fading light. “Sorry, sweeties,” he said, his voice laced with gentle amusement as he carefully removed his diving gear. “No such treasures as you imagined. But I found some jewels on the shore\!”  

Their eyes widened in disbelief. “Where? I want to see them\!”  

Amerigo pointed with two fingers at his offspring, his face beaming with pride. “You\!” he chuckled, his eyes twinkling with affection.  

Their laughter was a cascade of pure, unadulterated joy. They launched themselves from the shore, landing playfully on Amerigo. He stumbled backward, then toppled back into the river. All three bodies surfaced, spluttering and laughing. Their sudden, perfectly synchronized attack turned into a playful wrestling match, their movements so unified it caught Amerigo off guard, a flash of that earlier unsettling mystery briefly returning.  

Soon, Amerigo’s watch alarm sounded, a gentle chime that signaled the end of their aquatic frolic. The twins pouted but walked to the picnic blanket, their small feet making soft squelching noises in the damp sand. Before joining them, he retrieved the MRE from the shore and recycled the diving gear. He conjured three towels when he got to the blanket.  

Niamh had already dropped behind the western ridge, its yellow-white light fading. Only little Ciara’s dim orange light remained low in the sky, casting long, solitary shadows. The air cooled with the loss; the twin shadows of earlier times, now gone, stretched into single bluish lines across the ground. A pleasant chill settled over the landscape, and Amerigo, wrapped in a towel, watched his daughters, their small forms silhouetted against the fading light, feeling a profound peace. The puzzle of their connection, the unanswered questions, could wait for another time. For now, there was only this moment, this quiet, simple joy.  

Once they were dressed, Amerigo put the MRE to good use, efficiently recycling the blanket, towels, and dishes. As he bent to pack the unit in its case, his hand brushed against a small, heavy chunk of rock half-buried in the riverbank’s sand. He pulled it free, revealing a rough, metallic lump, its surface mottled with a silvery-gray sheen and faint iridescent streaks of blue and purple.  

Amerigo smiled, turning the bismuth ore over in his hands. It was a perfect specimen for the twins—its unique oxide layer would fascinate them, a chance to teach them about metallic properties and their applications. He imagined their eyes lighting up as he explained how such materials could conduct energy in unexpected ways. He tucked it into his pocket, eager to share it with them later.  

As he looked up, he spotted the autocab approaching in the distance, signaling the end of their idyllic riverside retreat.

En route to the spaceport, Amerigo indulged his daughters’ fascination with the little cave by describing its features. Remembering the sample in his pocket, he withdrew it and held it up to catch Ciara’s last remaining rays streaming through the autocab’s window, its iridescent streaks of blue and purple shimmering faintly. “Look at this, girls,” he said. “This is bismuth ore. See that rainbow sheen? That’s an oxide layer, formed when the metal reacts with air. It’s a great conductor, too—perfect for carrying electrical signals in advanced tech.”  

“That’s neat\!” Electra exclaimed, taking it from him and examining it before slipping it into a small purse they shared, her enthusiasm mirrored perfectly by Celeste. Then, they bombarded Amerigo with their boundless imagination, conjuring up the wonders that might lie hidden within the unexplored side tunnel.

Back on Utopis, the gravity ride to their home deck felt sluggish after the long shuttle trip from Ares. Celectra’s twin bodies ached faintly—the station’s pull never quite matched a planet’s. She hurried inside. The bismuth ore needed cleaning before she could study it properly.  

In the bathroom, her left body turned on the sink, letting a cool stream fill the pearlescent basin. The riverbank dust had dulled the bismuth’s shine, hiding the colors she’d glimpsed in the autocab. She held it beneath the water, brushing away grit with patient, circular strokes. 

Her right body tended to more personal business after the long trip, half listening to the hiss of water and the rhythmic tap of her other hand. It was comforting, the twin sounds syncing like heartbeat and breath. With two tasks completed, her bodies traded places, leaving the stone on the counter, one sitting while the other washed her hands.  

She dried her hands, then took the cleaned bismuth and carried it to the bedroom. The crystal gleamed faintly in the artificial light, already beginning to dry in the recycled air. Her other body stayed behind another minute or two.  

In the bedroom, she was already lying back on the bed with the bismuth resting on her chest. When her second body entered, she sat beside herself, both pairs of eyes turning to the crystal.  

Under her twin gaze, the lattice unfolded in radiant geometry. One set of eyes caught green sparks, the other violet veins. Their perceptions merged, forming a single, deeper image—depth layered on depth until the boundaries blurred.  

The bismuth’s structure reflected her own: two patterns, perfectly matched, impossible to separate. Its beauty was precise and mathematical but also lonely. She felt that same tension within herself—a harmony stretched too thin.  

The crystal gave no answers—only its quiet invitation to keep seeing everything at once, as if comprehension itself were the act of looking.  

Her right self dropped lightly to the floor, holding the bismuth. Her left lingered a moment longer, then reached down and tapped the storage panel beneath the bed. The hidden mechanism stirred to life, humming softly as the drawer extended and its lids parted like quiet wings. 

Her right hand placed the clean bismuth specimen in its empty cradle on the upper tier. For a heartbeat, both bodies leaned close, admiring the subtle shimmer before she closed the case. The wings folded shut, the drawer slid home, and the motor gave a contented sigh. She didn't know why she kept special specimens here, beneath her bed where she slept. Only that certain metals—bismuth, tellurium—felt... calming against her skin. Like they quieted the faint static that lived beneath her bones for as long as she could remember.  

Then she headed to the living room to do her homework, even though it was still her birthday.

Amerigo sat at the kitchen table with a cup of decaf, watching Celectra work through the math challenge Utara had assigned her as “advanced enrichment.” High school-level calculus—problems that made his own head spin.  

One body sketched equations in smooth, confident strokes across her holopad. The other drew geometric proofs, translating abstract numbers into visual shapes. Both worked simultaneously on different aspects of the same problem, never consulting, never checking with each other, never even glancing between bodies.  

She finished in twelve minutes. Both holopads chimed completion at the exact same instant. 

“That was fun\!” she said, both faces grinning up at him. “Can I have another?”  

Amerigo stared at the completed work, his scientist’s brain cataloging what he’d just witnessed. Not just the advanced mathematics—though that alone was remarkable for a seven-year-old. But the *coordination*. The seamless division of a single complex task across two bodies, working in perfect parallel like dual processors sharing one operating system.  

The tickling incident from earlier surfaced in his mind, along with a dozen other odd moments he’d filed away over the years. A nagging question breached the surface of his thoughts like a whale breaking through calm water: could their connection be more than mere twin intuition? Could they somehow share a single awareness, impossible as it seemed?  

He called the girls to set their holopads aside and settled them across from him at the table. His gaze moved between their two sets of identical milk chocolate eyes, curiosity and disbelief warring in his expression. He reached across the table and gently took their hands in his own. 

“I’ve been thinking about what happened today—at the river, just now with the math. The way you always seem to know what the other is feeling, what the other is doing.” He paused, choosing his words carefully. “It’s not just a guess, is it? Can you truly be one person? A single mind inhabiting two brains, two bodies?”  

The identical faces lit up with radiant smiles, their eyes sparkling with a blend of shock, joy, and relief. “Finally\!” they exclaimed in perfect unison. “You understand what I’ve been trying to tell you all along\!”  

Amerigo nodded slowly, absorbing their words. He remembered now—every time he'd corrected them ('you-two'), they'd insisted, 'I am only one person.' He'd dismissed it as childish stubbornness. But today, watching them solve calculus in perfect parallel without a single exchanged glance... he finally heard the truth in that singular 'I.' “I understood you,” he clarified, “but it’s a matter of believing, of fully comprehending such an extraordinary concept. I don’t think anyone has ever encountered, or even imagined, such a phenomenon before.”  

He paused, his gaze dropping to the holopads still glowing with her completed work, his mind reeling with the implications. This wasn’t just a curiosity; it was a revelation. It was something that needed to be understood, protected, and studied—but carefully, by people who would see his daughter as a person, not a puzzle.  

He looked up at his daughter—truly looked at *her*, singular. Those milk chocolate eyes that were watching him with perfectly synchronized attention.  

“My brown-eyed girl,” he said softly, testing how it felt. The correction was small, just one letter dropped, but it changed everything.  

Both faces lit up with radiant smiles, and he felt something shift in his chest—acceptance, wonder, love.  

He raised his head, a new resolve hardening his expression. “This changes everything,” he announced. “I’m scheduling an appointment with Dr. Cruiz right now.”
