The Founding Festival always ran late into the dusk, because that was the only time Celectra could come out bare-headed.

Uncle Doc's rules: wide-brimmed hat, UV sleeves, and the wraparound shades whenever Niamh was even thinking about the horizon. But Niamh had dropped behind the western ridge an hour ago, and the sky over New Eden was doing the thing Celectra loved best: deepening from violet to ink while the moons came out one by one. Bran first, orange and fat. Then Fionn, blue-white and sharp. Maeve last, rust-red and shy, low over the river.

"No hats," both of her voices said happily, as Daddy tied the number bibs on. He'd made them himself that afternoon, on the fabric printer. CARISCO—TEAM 7, in bright yellow.

"No hats, no long sleeves," Daddy agreed, grinning. "Festival rules. Sundown means freedom." He smoothed the bib down over her collarbones—both collarbones, both bodies, working left to right like he'd done it a thousand times, because he had.

She was wearing her new festival set, and she couldn't stop looking down at it. Mommy had tuned it on the holopad that afternoon—Mommy, the word she'd been given permission to use on her eighth birthday and which still felt like a present she got to open every day—and it was cryo-silk, the good stuff from Uncle Doc's original medical pattern, woven through with the little light threads that carried nothing but color. Right now both of her outfits were drifting through slow swirls of deep teal and midnight blue, the glow pooling softly in the folds, like wearing a piece of the sky that hadn't decided what it wanted to be yet. Half the colony was shimmering. Down by the lantern line, the Galinstanians who'd come up for the festival were clustered at the edge of the field, and Celectra could feel Kiri's bright, needling curiosity at the edge of her awareness—you celebrate the dark by filling it with light? Explain the logic—while beside her, Stan's presence pulsed its slow, deep, riverbank patience. Stan said, to no one in particular, in his slow river voice: "The dark was fine before the lanterns. The dark will be fine after. But the lanterns are good." Which was, as far as Celectra could tell, Stan's entire opinion of the Founding Festival, and also his entire opinion of most things.

"Lace check," Daddy said, crouching. "Tie check. And —" he held up a soft woven strap, wide and padded, the kind they used for the under-twelves—"tie check."

<!-- page -->

Celectra looked down the line of teams warming up on the grass. Mostly older kids. Kai Vasquez and Amor Okonkwo, who were eleven and tall as the gatepost and had already won the relay that morning. Chen Wei and Leila Al-Farsi, who practiced, actually practiced, at the activity center. She was the smallest one entered. Maybe the smallest one entered ever, because everyone knew the sensible way to run a three-legged race was with a partner, and her partner had walked down to the field with her, wearing the same face.

Both her bodies sat side by side on the grass and let Daddy bind their inner legs together—left body's right ankle to right body's left ankle, snug over the cryo-silk, which gave exactly the way it was engineered to and then settled back, cool against her skin where the night air had bitten in.

"Sure you don't want a real partner, little star?" called Amara Voss, the judge, with a kind laugh. "You could trade one of yourselves in."

"They're my best partner," Celectra said, both voices, perfectly flat, and Amara laughed so hard she nearly dropped the flag.

The whistle blew.

And the thing was—and Celectra had never once had to explain this, because how could you explain breathing?—the thing was that her legs already knew each other. There was no negotiation. Left body's bound leg swung forward because right body's bound leg swung forward, at the same instant, from the same thought, the way one bell is one tone no matter which side of it you strike. Inner-inner-out. Inner-inner-out. Three legs moving like four, their joined stride as smooth as a wheel, and the only sound her team made on the grass was a single soft whish, whish, whish.

<!-- page -->

She passed Kai and Amor before the first turn cone. They were fast, but they were also two people, and two people breathe two counts, and count two different strides, and spend the whole race shouting MY side, YOUR side at each other. Celectra's team didn't shout. Celectra's team just leaned into the turn like one small moon going around one small planet, and came off the cone with both bodies' weight perfectly shared, and blew past Chen Wei and Leila on the back straight while the crowd started doing that thing where it gets loud in a rising wave.

She crossed the line first, both hands up, both bodies' lungs full of the same cold sweet air, and the cryo-silk on her chest was swirling bright green and gold now, because Mommy was clapping in the stands and the outfits, Mommy had confessed once, took a cue from the wearer's heartbeat. Mommy had not confessed the part where she could override it from the holopad. Celectra had figured that out on her own at winter solstice, when her pajamas turned candy-striped for no reason she'd been told about.

"Time!" Amara Voss shouted, and read it off the field clock, and whistled low. "That's a colony junior record, folks. By nine seconds."

"Nine seconds," Daddy repeated faintly, when both of Celectra's bodies barreled into him, which made four arms and one hug. "Nine—sweetheart, that's not a junior record, that's the open record."

"I know," she said, and then, because she was nine and honest: "It's easier for me. Nobody has to learn anybody."

That was when Kiri arrived at the rope, all four of her eyes bright as struck flint.

<!-- page -->

"You won," Kiri said, "by being one. So why did the small one need a partner at all? If you are one, you are a partner to yourself. The rule seems to me—" she clicked something in her throat, a Galinstanian word that didn't translate "—inefficient."

"Because the race is for teams," Celectra said.

"The race is for two people," Kiri said. "You are one person. The race is wrong about you. Why do you keep entering it?"

And that was when Councilor Okafor—who had been listening, and who judged the Athletics Committee the way other people judged pie contests, only louder—raised his hand at the judge's table.

"Amara! Second heat's got an open slot. Re-pair the champions. One body to a team—pick her two partners from the runners-up."

Daddy opened his mouth. Mommy, appearing beside him with iced tea and a look, closed it for him with one raised eyebrow.

<!-- page -->

Celectra's stomach did something small and complicated. Kiri, four eyes bright, looked delighted, which was somehow worse.

The draw put her left body with Aulii Vongphachanh and her right body with Kai Vasquez. Which meant, she realized as they knelt on the grass and the padded straps went on, that she was now two teams. Both her bodies were bound to other people's legs, in different lanes, facing the same direction but unable to see each other—and her mind, her one mind, sat exactly in the middle.

The whistle blew, and it was like trying to hear two conversations at once, if each conversation were happening in her own knees.

Aulii counted loud—one-two, one-two—a marching rhythm, metronome-steady, technically perfect, and Celectra's left body fell into it fine for six strides. But six strides was exactly as long as Kai lasted on her other side before he lurched into a scrambling sprinter's gait, fast and ragged, and here was the problem, the real problem, the one she couldn't have explained to Councilor Okafor if she'd tried: her bodies matched each other. Not on purpose. They matched each other the way two heartbeats in one chest matched each other. Every time Kai surged, her right body surged, and two meters away her left body surged with it, out of Aulii's count.

Aulii stumbled. Shouted. The strap bit.

They went down in the grass in a tangle of four legs that only belonged to two rhythms—left body and Aulii, a heap of elbows, Aulii's cheek in the dirt and her count still going, one-two, one-two, out of sheer stubbornness.

<!-- page -->

Celectra got them up. It took her a second to work out how, because she was still running the other team, and the other team couldn't stop. Her right body was still bound to Kai, and Kai was still going, and if she stopped the right body cold, Kai would go over his own feet. So she left the right body running—gave it a rhythm to hold, four counts, steady, don't look down—and used the left body to do the rescue. Free leg planted. Bound leg as a pivot. Both left arms hauling, one under Aulii's elbow and one fisted in the back of her shirt, and she pulled with everything the left body had while the right body kept counting four-four-four and Kai kept running and did not notice, for three whole strides, that anything had happened.

They were up. Aulii was up. The right body was up.

Then her right body hitched—just a hitch, the sympathetic flinch of one body feeling the other's effort—and Kai, who had been running on his own count, went down like a dropped coat.

In the stands, Daddy half-rose. Mommy's hand closed around his wrist and did not let go.

She got him up too. This time she had nothing to pivot against and nobody to haul but Kai, who was heavier than he looked and sulking already, and she had to do it while the left body ran, while Aulii's metronome counted, and the only way she could find the leverage was to borrow the left body's count and count it into the right body's knees—one, plant, two, pull, three, up—which worked, technically, in the sense that Kai ended up vertical, but it meant Aulii spent four strides running beside a partner whose mind was two meters behind her, and Aulii's perfect metronome wavered, and Celectra felt the wobble travel up the strap like a struck wire.

They didn't fall again. That was the only good thing that could be said about the rest of the heat.

<!-- page -->

She tried—she split herself thin, the way she split attention when one body read and one body built, but reading and building didn't fight each other the way two partners' strides did. Her own bodies' perfect sympathy, the thing that had won the first race by nine seconds, was the exact thing dragging both teams sideways. She was the wrong kind of partner: the kind whose other partner was always, already there.

Chen Wei and Amor won the second heat. Aulii and Celectra came in fifth of six. Kai and Celectra came in sixth, mostly because Kai was sulking, and sulking, it turned out, was very hard to run in.

They sat in a row on the grass afterward, all five kids, breathing. Celectra's outfits had gone a quiet, honest gray-blue.

"You're not bad," Aulii said at last, elbows on knees. "You're just—synchronized. With yourself. It's weird. It's like racing someone who's already three steps into my next step before I am."

"You are," Celectra admitted. "Sorry. I don't know how to be unsynchronized."

"Well," Aulii said, considering, "next year you could teach me. Then we'd both be weird."

<!-- page -->

Above the river, the first firework went up.

It cracked open gold over the water, and the whole crowd's ooh rolled across the field, and Kiri's voice cut sharp across the grass—"Again! Do the big one again!"—bright as cut crystal.

Stan came up the field slowly, the way Stan did everything, his presence arriving before his body the way a river arrives before its water. He stopped beside the row of kids in the grass and looked down at them with the patience of a bank.

"The small one is sad," he observed, "because she did not win a race that was not built for her."

"I'm not sad," Celectra said, which was not convincing.

"You are sad," Stan said, agreeable as weather. "It is a good sadness. It means you have noticed the shape of the container." He settled onto the grass—slowly, in stages, like a hill deciding to sit down—and looked out at the river. "In my river, we do not race. We swim, and the ones who swim alone swim alone, and the ones who swim together swim together, and no one has ever thought to ask which is faster. The river is not a race. The river is a river."

<!-- page -->

"That's very wise," said Mommy, who had come up behind them with her hands full of iced tea and her PsyD folded away somewhere behind her face, "and it's also exactly what I've been saying to the Athletics Committee for three years. Synchrony isn't a deficit. It's a different baseline. The test was normed on somebody else."

Stan turned one slow eye on her. "The mother understands rivers."

"The mother understands normed tests," Mommy said. "But yes."

Celectra tipped both heads back against Daddy's shoulders—one shoulder each, which was the best configuration—and watched the sky come apart in rings of green and violet while her cryo-silk did its best to keep up, swirls of reflected festival light chasing the bursts across her chest, sleeve to sleeve, like the night had finally found something worth copying.

"I should practice with other people for next year," one body said to Daddy.

"And with myself," said the other.

<!-- page -->

"I'm not cut out for last place," both voices agreed, and the third firework—a big one, pink and purple, Daddy's telltale ordering from the pyro cart because she could see him smiling at the sky like he knew what she was thinking—bloomed over the river, and everything on the field, cloth and skin and water, turned briefly the color of her room.
