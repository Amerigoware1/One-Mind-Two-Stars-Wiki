“Bedtime, young lady,” Daddy said, mock-stern, his eyes soft.

Celectra wrinkled her noses. “Only if Zoë tucks me in too. Both of you.” Her voices carried a playful challenge, her eyes glinting.

Daddy raised an eyebrow. “Scheming.”

“Am not,” she said, grinning as she slipped back into her nighties, movements smooth and mirrored.

She tugged Zoë and Daddy’s hands, pulling them to the bedroom. Uncle Doc followed, leaning against the bedroom doorway with a fond smile, his med-kit slung over his shoulder, content to watch unobtrusively.

Both bodies scrambled under the blanket, leaving space on the mattress. Through the open door, past Uncle Doc, the living space pulsed with the lights of the twin stars, Chaos and Ares, through the porthole in the station’s spin, a rhythm that lulled her.

<!-- page -->

Zoë smoothed Celectra’s hair, her touch gentle. Daddy tucked the blanket under her chins, his fingers careful. Their movements aligned without effort, a quiet dance of care that warmed Celectra’s dual hearts.

“See?” Celectra mumbled, yawning, her voices soft. “You fit.” Her eyes, half-closed, caught their closeness, a warmth she felt deep in her chest.

Daddy glanced at Zoë, his expression tender. Zoë’s smile glowed, a feeling Celectra sensed without seeing.

“Daddy? Tell me about the journey. From Earth.”

He settled on the edge of the bed, Zoë on the other side. Uncle Doc was still leaning against the doorframe. “You’ve heard this before, sweetheart.”

“I know. But I like hearing it.” Both her faces looked up at him in the soft blue glow. “Especially the part about Mommy-Jenni.”

<!-- page -->

He smiled, the old ache softened by time and Zoë’s presence. “Okay. But then sleep, deal?”

“Deal.”

“The Utopis left Earth orbit a hundred and seven years ago,” he began, falling into the familiar rhythm. “Three hundred colonists in cryosleep. Your mommy and I went under holding hands—they let us share adjacent pods.”

“Seventy-eight years,” Celectra whispered. “But ninety for Earth.”

“Right. Time dilation. We accelerated for six months until we hit 0.5c—half the speed of light. At those speeds, time slows down for the travelers.” He brushed hair from one of her foreheads. “We passed three star systems during the acceleration phase.”

“Ross 128,” she recited. “Wolf 1061\. Gliese 832.”

<!-- page -->

“Good memory. We couldn’t stop—too much momentum—but Utara scanned them as we passed. Left beacons. Ross 128 had biosignatures, maybe life. Wolf 1061 had violent tectonics. The probe we deployed lasted six months before going silent. Gliese 832 looked promising; it made Utara consider stopping there.”

“Why didn’t she?”

“We’d already committed to Niamh-Ciara. The fuel, the trajectory—changing course would’ve stranded us between stars.” He paused. “Besides, this system had something special. A warm super-Jupiter with habitable moons. A place where we could build something new.”

“But, you weren’t frozen popsicles.” Celectra looked at Zoë.

Zoë smiled and kissed Celectra’s closest forehead. “No, sweetheart, not frozen popsicles. Just about one degree Celsius. We used pharmacological metabolic stasis—it slowed everything down to a tiny whisper without turning us into ice. That’s why the EMS could still give our muscles little workouts.”

Celectra asked through a yawn, “What was it like, waking up?”

<!-- page -->

Amerigo’s expression grew distant. “Disorienting. The pod’s viewport was frosted, and this mechanical voice counted down your revival. Forty-five light-years from home, and you haven’t aged a day while Earth has aged ninety. Everyone you knew is gone or ancient.

“We spent a year rehabilitating. Even constant 0.9-*g* and electrical muscle stimulation weren’t enough to keep our bodies from deteriorating somewhat.

“But Mommy-Jenni was there,” Celectra prompted.

“She was able to get up an hour before me. When I found her in the observation lounge, she was staring at the binary suns and all the wonders you see every day. She turned, smiled, and said, ‘We made it. We actually made it.’” His voice thickened. “We held each other while the ship settled in orbit. Two people among three hundred who’d gambled everything on this one-way journey.”

“Not everyone made it, though,” Celectra said quietly.

“No. Equipment failures, radiation exposure. By the time we arrived, only two hundred and ninety-six colonists had survived.” He squeezed her hand. “But your mommy did. And because she did, I have you.”

<!-- page -->

Zoë, on the other side of the bed, kissed Celectra’s other forehead. “And now you have both of us.” “Both of you,” Celectra agreed, her eyes drifting closed. “My family galaxy.”

“Sleep now,” Amerigo whispered. “Dream of stars.”

“Always do,” she murmured, already fading.

Uncle Doc slipped out quietly, his soft chuckle echoing in the hall, leaving them to their moment. Their soft murmurs lingered as they stayed by her side—no longer awkward, just together. Celectra drifted to sleep, certain she was home.
